Budget 2026's 'Mega Deduction': write off most new equipment in year one (proposed)

By Calcitax Advisors · November 14, 2026

The federal government is promoting a proposal in Budget 2026 that would let businesses deduct the full cost of most new equipment, machinery and technology in the year it is put to use, instead of spreading the deduction over many years. Called the Productivity Mega Deduction, it would apply to most eligible property bought on or after September 15, 2026, and it is meant to be permanent. It is not law yet.

What happened

Finance Canada first announced the Productivity Mega Deduction on September 15, 2026, and Budget 2026, tabled on November 5, includes it. On November 13, 2026, the government issued news releases highlighting it as a key measure for businesses of all sizes, including small businesses buying new equipment and technology.

Normally, when a business buys a long-lasting asset such as a machine, computer or piece of equipment, it cannot deduct the whole cost right away. Instead it claims capital cost allowance (CCA), which is tax depreciation: a percentage of the cost each year, set by the asset's CCA "class". Under the proposal, the full cost of eligible property could be deducted in the year it becomes available for use, meaning installed and ready to work, not just ordered or paid for. This is called immediate expensing.

Finance Canada says the measure would cover about two-thirds of business investment in capital assets, compared with about 15% under the narrower Productivity Super-Deduction from Budget 2025. It estimates the cost at $36 billion over five years.

What qualifies, and what doesn't

According to Finance Canada's September announcement, most depreciable property acquired on or after September 15, 2026 would qualify. The main exclusions are:

  • buildings and building additions in CCA Classes 1 and 3 (manufacturing and processing buildings keep the separate, temporary immediate expensing announced in Budget 2025);
  • Classes 14 and 14.1, which include franchises, licences and goodwill;
  • certain vehicles in Classes 10 and 10.1 (the accounting firm Welch LLP notes this catches passenger vehicles);
  • Class 51 regulated natural gas distribution pipelines and a few other specialized types of property.

There are also limits on used property. A used asset qualifies only if neither you nor anyone not at arm's length with you (for example, a relative or a company you control) owned it before, and it wasn't transferred to you through a tax-deferred rollover.

Property that doesn't qualify can still use the existing, temporary Accelerated Investment Incentive, which allows a larger CCA claim in the first year than usual.

What it means for small businesses

  • Corporations buying eligible equipment, tools, computers or software could reduce their taxable income by the full cost in the first year, which frees up cash for growth.
  • Sole proprietors and partnerships with individual partners should note that Finance Canada says there would be rules stopping individuals from using the deduction to create or increase a loss. These mirror the temporary small-business immediate expensing rules from 2021. In practice, the deduction may be capped by your business income for the year.
  • A full write-off isn't always best. Deducting more now leaves less to deduct later, and it can lower your income in a year when your tax rate is already low. Claiming CCA is optional, so you can usually choose how much to claim.
  • Selling the asset later can create "recapture", which means some or all of the earlier deduction is added back to income.

What it means for you as an individual

If you don't run a business, this measure doesn't change your personal taxes. If you earn self-employment income, for example as a contractor or gig worker, it could apply to equipment you buy for that work, subject to the loss rules above. What to do now

  • Remember it is proposed. Budget measures only become law once Parliament passes them. Draft legislation has been released, but details can still change.
  • Timing matters at year end. If your business plans to buy equipment, the deduction would depend on when it is available for use, not when you pay. Something delivered but not set up until January would generally belong to next year.
  • Keep good records: invoices, delivery and installation dates, and whether the item is new or used and who owned it before.
  • Don't buy just for the deduction. A deduction saves tax at your tax rate; it doesn't make the purchase free.
  • Talk to your accountant before your year end about which CCA class your purchases fall into and how much to claim. Our sole proprietor vs. incorporation guide covers how business structure affects taxes.
