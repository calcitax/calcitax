Budget 2026 mortgage changes: what's proposed for home buyers and small builders

By Calcitax Advisors · November 15, 2026

The federal budget tabled on November 5, 2026 includes a group of mortgage and housing measures. Most are proposals that still need legislation or new regulations, but one set of changes for three- to fifty-unit buildings is scheduled to start on February 1, 2027. Here is what each one means for buyers, owners and small builders in Metro Vancouver.

What happened

Budget 2026 ("Masters of our destiny") lists these housing and mortgage measures:

  • A higher price limit for optional mortgage insurance (proposed). The limit would rise from $1 million to $1.5 million, and these insured mortgages could be paid off over 30 years when at least one borrower is a first-time buyer or the home is newly built.
  • Clearer mortgage rates (proposed). New regulations would have the Bank of Canada publish more information on insured mortgage rates, so borrowers can compare offers.
  • CRA income checks for lenders (proposed). The budget would let the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) sign agreements with lenders and mortgage insurers to confirm a borrower's income, to cut down on fraud. It sets aside $13 million over three years, starting in 2027-28, to build and test the service.
  • Prepayment penalties (consultation only). The government plans to consult on making penalties for paying off an insured mortgage early easier to understand. Nothing has changed yet.
  • The foreign buyer ban (proposed extension). The ban on non-Canadians buying existing homes would be extended by two years, to January 1, 2029. Newly built homes would be exempt. Legislation is still to come.

What "optional" mortgage insurance means

Mortgage insurance protects the lender, not you, if a borrower stops paying. If your down payment is under 20%, your mortgage is insured, and the price cap for those mortgages already went up to $1.5 million on December 15, 2024, along with 30-year amortizations (the time to pay off the loan) for first-time buyers and buyers of new builds.

When you put down 20% or more, the lender may still choose to insure the loan. That is the "optional" insurance in this proposal. Insuring the loan protects the lender and can help it offer you a lower rate. Today that option stops at homes priced at $1 million. The budget would raise it to $1.5 million. No start date has been announced.

What it means for you

  • Buyers with 20% down on homes between $1 million and $1.5 million may see more lenders competing for their mortgage once the change takes effect. It does not change how much you must put down.
  • Anyone renewing or shopping for a mortgage should eventually find it easier to compare insured rates. Until the new rules arrive, compare offers yourself.
  • Newcomers and temporary residents should note the foreign buyer ban is set to end on January 1, 2027 under current law. The extension to 2029 is only proposed, so check the rules with a lawyer before signing anything.

Remember that a longer amortization lowers your monthly payment but means you pay more interest over the life of the loan.

What it means for small builders and landlords

From February 1, 2027, the government plans to loosen mortgage insurance rules for "missing middle" housing:

  • New three- and four-unit homes: builders could get insured construction loans of up to 90% of the property's value, with no maximum property value and no rule that the builder must live in one unit. Once built, the units could be kept as rentals with an insured mortgage amortized over up to 30 years, or sold.
  • Five- to fifty-unit buildings: private mortgage insurers would be allowed to insure these properties. The Department of Finance has released a technical backgrounder with the details, so ask your lender how it applies to your project.

If you build or buy units to rent, keep in mind that rent is taxable income and that the cost of the building is written off over time through capital cost allowance (a yearly tax deduction for wear and tear). Our rental income tax guide explains the basics, and our guide to selling a home or investment property covers what happens when you sell.

What to do now

  • Don't rush a purchase because of a proposal. Most of these measures have no start date yet.
  • If you are a first-time buyer, keep using the tools already in place, such as the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan. Our RRSP, TFSA and FHSA guide explains how they work.
  • If you are planning a triplex, fourplex or small apartment project, talk to your lender about the February 1, 2027 changes and keep good records of your building costs from day one.
  • Watch for the legislation. We will report when these measures become law.
