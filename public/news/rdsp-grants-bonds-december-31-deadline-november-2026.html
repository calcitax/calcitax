RDSP grants and bonds: contribute by December 31 to get 2026's government money

By Calcitax Advisors · November 9, 2026

If you or someone in your family has a Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP), December 31, 2026 is the last day to put money in and have it count for this year's government grant. The federal government can add up to $3,500 in grants and $1,000 in bonds for 2026, and many families leave some of that on the table.

What is an RDSP?

An RDSP is a long-term savings plan for a person who is approved for the disability tax credit (DTC). The person the plan is for is called the beneficiary. The plan holder (often a parent for a child) opens it at a bank or credit union, and anyone can contribute with the holder's written permission.

Contributions are not tax-deductible, but the money grows inside the plan without yearly tax. The real attraction is the free government money that comes with it:

  • Canada Disability Savings Grant: the government matches your contributions.
  • Canada Disability Savings Bond: extra money for lower-income families, paid even if you contribute nothing.

To open a plan, the beneficiary must be a resident of Canada with a Social Insurance Number, be approved for the DTC, and apply before December 31 of the year they turn 59. If you are not sure whether you qualify for the DTC, our guide to often-missed tax credits explains how it works.

How much the government adds for 2026

The grant depends on family income. For 2026, Employment and Social Development Canada uses income from the 2024 tax return:

  • Family income of $117,045 or less: the government adds $3 for each of the first $500 you contribute, then $2 for each of the next $1,000. Contributing $1,500 in the year gets the maximum grant of $3,500.
  • Family income above $117,045: the government matches $1 for $1 on up to $1,000, so contributing $1,000 gets a $1,000 grant.

The bond needs no contribution at all:

  • Family income of $38,237 or less: the full $1,000 bond.
  • Between $38,237 and $58,523: a smaller bond that shrinks as income rises.
  • $58,523 or more: no bond.

There are lifetime limits of $70,000 in grants, $20,000 in bonds and $200,000 in total contributions. Grants and bonds stop after December 31 of the year the beneficiary turns 49.

Why December 31 matters

The grant is based on what you put in during the calendar year. Unlike an RRSP, there is no extra 60-day window in the new year. A contribution made on January 2, 2027 counts toward 2027, not 2026.

This matters most in two situations:

  • The beneficiary turns 49 in 2026. Only contributions made by December 31, 2026 can earn a grant. After that, no more grants or bonds are paid, although the plan can stay open.
  • You haven't contributed yet this year. If you skip a year, you can usually catch up later, but the rules have limits (see below), and the money starts growing later.

Catching up on missed years

If the beneficiary was eligible for the DTC in earlier years but you didn't contribute, unused grant and bond entitlements from the past 10 years can be carried forward. While you are catching up, the grant can be as high as $10,500 in one year, and a new plan can receive up to $11,000 in bonds (up to $1,000 for each eligible year in the last 10, plus the current year). Catch-up grants still need contributions; catch-up bonds don't.

The 10-year window keeps moving, so entitlement from the oldest year drops off each year you wait.

What it means for families

  • File tax returns every year. Grants and bonds are based on family income from filed returns. For a child under 18, that's the parents' or guardians' returns. The CRA says the beneficiary should start filing their own returns in the year they turn 17 so the amounts stay correct when the income test switches to their own income.
  • Think long term. If you withdraw money, you generally have to pay back $3 of grants and bonds received in the previous 10 years for every $1 you take out (up to the total received in those 10 years). Closing the plan triggers full repayment of that 10-year amount. An RDSP works best as money left alone for the future.

What to do now

  1. Check that the beneficiary's DTC approval is in place. Without it, no new contributions, grants or bonds are allowed.
  2. Look at your RDSP statement or ask your financial institution how much has gone in for 2026 so far.
  3. If your family income is $117,045 or less, aim for $1,500 by December 31; above that, $1,000 gets the full matching grant.
  4. Don't leave it to the last week of December: banks need time to process deposits before year end.
  5. Make sure everyone whose income counts has filed their 2024 and 2025 returns.

If you haven't opened an RDSP yet but the beneficiary qualifies, opening one now also starts the bond and catch-up process. Ask us if you'd like help checking DTC eligibility or filing missed returns.
