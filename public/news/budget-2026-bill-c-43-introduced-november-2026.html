Budget 2026 bill introduced: what Bill C-43 would change, and what isn't law yet

By Calcitax Advisors · November 27, 2026

On November 26, 2026, Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne introduced Bill C-43, the first bill to put measures from the November 5 federal budget into law. It covers fewer tax instalments, simpler medical expense claims, bigger business write-offs and more. Nothing in it is law yet: Parliament still has to pass it.

What happened

A federal budget is a plan. To change tax rules, the government has to pass a law, usually called a budget implementation act. Bill C-43 is the first one for Budget 2026. Finance Canada describes each item as proposed in the bill. Some budget measures, such as the plan to move the tax filing deadline to May 15, are not named in Finance Canada's announcement of this bill, so don't assume every budget promise is covered by it.

If you missed the budget itself, see our overview of Budget 2026.

What it means for you

  • Fewer people would pay tax instalments. Instalments are payments you make during the year when not enough tax is taken off at source, often because you're self-employed, a landlord or an investor. Today you generally have to pay them if your net tax owing (federal plus provincial, outside Quebec) is more than $3,000. The budget proposes raising that threshold to $7,500 for individuals and corporations outside Quebec, indexed to inflation, starting with taxation years that begin on or after January 1, 2028. Finance Canada says nearly 1.1 million Canadians and 185,000 small businesses would benefit.
  • Self-employed people could make one payment a year. For 2028 and later, people whose main income is from a business could choose a single instalment equal to two-thirds of their net tax owing, due by December 31, instead of quarterly payments.
  • Easier medical expense claims. For some items, such as insulin and certain breathing devices, the budget proposes removing the need for a prescription, and for others it would accept certification from an authorized medical practitioner instead. Finance Canada's budget document says this would apply from the 2026 tax year. The bill also covers simpler documentation for people with celiac disease.
  • One credit for volunteer firefighters and search and rescue volunteers. Today there are two separate 14% credits on $6,000, and you can claim only one. The budget proposes a single 14% credit on $6,000, with hours from both kinds of service counting toward the 200-hour minimum, from the 2027 tax year.
  • A larger workers benefit. The bill would strengthen the Build Canada Workers Benefit, a refundable credit for lower-income workers, so that more people qualify and payments rise.
  • Disability supports. The bill would simplify Disability Tax Credit applications for certain long-lasting conditions and permanently allow eligible family members to open a Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP) for an adult who can't open one alone.
  • Apprentice mechanics. Apprentice vehicle mechanics would be able to deduct all eligible tool costs under their special deduction from the 2027 tax year, instead of only the portion above a threshold.

What it means for small businesses

  • Productivity Mega Deduction. The bill would let businesses of all sizes immediately deduct the full cost of most new capital investments, on a permanent basis. Read our earlier article on how the Mega Deduction works.
  • Family farm transfers. More family members would qualify for tax-deferred intergenerational farm rollovers. The budget document extends this to nieces, nephews, grandnieces and grandnephews for transfers on or after January 1, 2027.
  • GST/HST fraud rules. New rules to fight "carousel" fraud (a scheme where GST/HST is claimed back but never paid) would start with certain telecommunications transactions.
  • Fewer instalments for incorporated businesses, under the same $7,500 threshold described above.

What hasn't changed yet

Until the bill passes and receives Royal Assent, current rules apply. That means:

  • If the CRA sends you an instalment reminder, the $3,000 threshold still applies for 2026 and 2027. Our tax instalments guide explains how to work out what you owe. The December 15, 2026 instalment is still due.
  • The tax filing deadline for your 2026 return is still April 30, 2027 (June 15, 2027 if you or your spouse are self-employed, with any balance due April 30). See our article on the proposed May 15 deadline.
  • Bills can be amended in Parliament, so details may still change.

What to do now

  • Keep paying instalments as requested by the CRA. The higher threshold, if passed, would not start until 2028.
  • Business owners planning equipment purchases should talk to an accountant about timing, since the Mega Deduction rules are proposed and still before Parliament.
  • Keep your medical receipts as usual. The simpler documentation rules would help, but you still need proof of what you paid.
  • Watch for updates. We'll report when the bill passes or changes.
