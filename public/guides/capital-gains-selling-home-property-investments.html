Capital gains in Canada: selling your home, a rental property or investments

Selling a home, a rental condo or some shares can bring a large profit. Here's how the CRA taxes that profit, when your home is tax-free, and the reporting steps families and small landlords often miss.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 19, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Only half of a capital gain is taxed; the planned two-thirds rate was cancelled in March 2025.
  • The gain is the sale price minus your adjusted cost base and selling costs.
  • The principal residence exemption can make your home's gain tax-free, but you must report the sale on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND).
  • Homes owned under 365 days can be fully taxed as business income, and B.C. adds a flipping tax for homes owned under 730 days.
  • Net capital losses can go back three years or forward indefinitely, against capital gains.
  • For a 2026 loss on Canadian stocks or ETFs, the sale must settle by December 31, 2026, so trade by about December 30.

What a capital gain is, in plain words

When you sell something you own as an investment, such as shares, a mutual fund, a rental condo or a piece of land, the CRA calls it capital property. If you sell it for more than it cost you, the profit is a capital gain. If you sell it for less, it's a capital loss.

Three terms matter:

  • Proceeds of disposition: what you get for the property, usually the sale price.
  • Adjusted cost base (ACB): what the property cost you, plus the costs of buying it (such as commissions and legal fees), plus improvements that add lasting value. Normal repairs and upkeep don't count.
  • Outlays and expenses: the costs of selling, such as real estate commissions, legal fees, advertising and surveyor's fees.

Capital gain = proceeds − (ACB + outlays and expenses)

Illustration only: you bought shares for $10,000, including the trading fee. Years later you sell them for $16,000 and pay $100 in fees to sell. Your capital gain is $16,000 − ($10,000 + $100) = $5,900.

You report the sale on Schedule 3 of your tax return. The taxable part ends up on line 12700.

How much of the gain is taxed in 2026

You don't pay tax on the whole gain. Only part of it, called the taxable capital gain, is added to your income. The share that's taxed is called the inclusion rate.

For 2026 the inclusion rate is one-half (50%). In the example above, half of the $5,900 gain, or $2,950, is added to your income for the year.

The two-thirds increase is not happening. In 2024 the federal government proposed raising the inclusion rate to two-thirds, and in January 2025 it delayed the start to January 1, 2026. On March 21, 2025, the Prime Minister announced that the increase was cancelled. The federal budget tabled on November 5, 2026 still refers to the one-half rate.

A separate break, the lifetime capital gains exemption, can shelter gains on qualifying small business shares and farm or fishing property. Its limit rose to $1.25 million for qualifying sales on or after June 25, 2024. That increase became law in March 2026, and the limit is set to rise with inflation starting in 2026. It doesn't apply to your home, a rental property or publicly traded shares.

New and proposed: Budget 2026 proposes a separate exemption, the Canada Growth Incentive, on up to $15 million of gains on qualifying shares in Canadian companies issued on or after November 5, 2026 and held at least 36 months. It is not law yet.

Selling your home: the principal residence exemption

The principal residence exemption can make the gain on selling it fully tax-free. A principal residence can be a house, condo, apartment, cottage, trailer, mobile home or even a houseboat.

Which home qualifies

For each year, the home can count as your principal residence if:

  • you own it, alone or with someone else,
  • you, your spouse or common-law partner (or former one), or any of your children lived in it at some time in that year, and
  • you designate it, meaning you formally name it as your principal residence for those years when you sell.

For 1982 and later years, a family can designate only one home per year. "Family" here generally means you, your spouse or partner and your children who are under 18 and not married or in a common-law relationship. The land that comes with the home is usually limited to half a hectare (about 1.24 acres).

You must report the sale, even if no tax is owed

Since the 2016 tax year, you must report the sale of your home on your return for the year you sell, using Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND). If the home was your principal residence for every year you owned it, or every year but one, you only fill in page 1 of the form.

Warning: if you forget, ask the CRA to change that year's return right away. A late designation can cost $100 for each full month it's late, up to $8,000.

When only part of the gain is exempt

If the home wasn't your principal residence for every year you owned it, only part of the gain is tax-free. The exempt part is worked out like this:

Gain × (1 + years designated) ÷ years owned

The "plus one" covers the year you sell one home and buy another. It only counts if you were a resident of Canada in the year you bought the home. Newcomers who bought a home before moving here should read our newcomers' tax guide.

Illustration only: you owned a condo for 10 years and designate it for 7 of them. The exempt share is (1 + 7) ÷ 10, or 80%. On a $100,000 gain, $80,000 is tax-free and $20,000 is a capital gain, half of which is taxed.

A loss on selling your home can't be claimed.

Selling a rental property, suite or second home

A rental, an investment property or a second home you don't designate is taxed like other capital property: half the gain is taxed.

  • Split land and building. When you sell a rental, you split the sale price between land and building and report each part separately on Schedule 3.
  • Watch for recapture. If you claimed capital cost allowance (the tax version of depreciation) on the building, some or all of it can be added back to your income in the year of sale. This is fully taxable, not half. Our rental income guide explains how this works.
  • Part of your home rented? Renting out a small part of your home usually doesn't affect the exemption, as long as you make no structural changes and claim no capital cost allowance on it. If you do either, part of your gain may be taxable.
  • Moved out and rented the home? A change in use is treated as if you sold the home at its fair market value (what a buyer would pay at the time) and bought it back at that price.
  • Home and cottage? Your family picks which one to designate for each year, so compare the numbers before you file.

Selling soon after buying: federal and B.C. flipping rules

Two separate sets of rules can apply to a quick sale, sometimes both at once.

Federal residential property flipping rule

For sales on or after January 1, 2023, if you owned a housing unit in Canada (including a rental unit or a presale contract) for less than 365 days in a row, any profit is treated as business income. That means it's fully taxable, not half, and the principal residence exemption isn't available. A loss on a flipped property is treated as zero.

The rule doesn't apply if you sold because of, or expecting, certain life events affecting you or your family, including:

  • a death, or a relative joining your household (or you joining theirs),
  • the breakdown of a marriage or common-law relationship, if you lived apart for at least 90 days before the sale,
  • a threat to your safety, or a serious illness or disability,
  • losing your job against your will, or moving for a new job, business or full-time post-secondary studies,
  • insolvency, or the home being destroyed or expropriated (taken by a government).

B.C. home flipping tax

Since January 1, 2025, B.C. has its own tax on profits from homes owned for less than 730 days.

  • The rate is 20% of the taxable profit if you sell within 365 days of buying.
  • Over the following 365 days, the rate shrinks gradually, reaching zero at 730 days.
  • If you lived in the home as your primary residence and owned it for at least 365 days in a row, you may be able to deduct up to $20,000.
  • If the tax applies, you must file a separate B.C. return within 90 days of the sale.

Selling shares, funds and other investments

Gains on shares, ETFs and mutual funds held outside a registered plan follow the same formula. Your broker may send a T5008 slip or an account statement showing the sale, but getting the ACB right is your job.

  • Average your cost. If you bought the same shares or fund units at different times and prices, they are identical properties. Your ACB is the average cost per unit, recalculated each time you buy more, not the price of the batch you think you sold.
  • Superficial loss rule. If you sell at a loss and you, your spouse or partner, or a corporation either of you controls buys the same investment within 30 days before or after the sale, and still owns it 30 days after, you can't claim the loss. The loss is usually added to the ACB of the shares bought back instead.
  • Foreign currency. Report everything in Canadian dollars. Convert the sale price using the exchange rate at the time of the sale.
  • Paid over time? If the buyer pays you over several years, you may be able to claim a reserve, which usually spreads the gain over up to five years.

Using capital losses

Half of a capital loss is an allowable capital loss. You first use it against your taxable capital gains in the same year. If losses are bigger than gains, the leftover is a net capital loss.

  • You can carry a net capital loss back three years to get back tax you paid on gains, using Form T1A. Don't file an amended return for this.
  • You can carry it forward with no time limit.
  • It can only reduce taxable capital gains, not your job or rental income.
  • Losses on personal-use property, such as your home, car or furniture, can't be claimed.

Tip: keep a record of unused net capital losses from past years. They can reduce or wipe out tax on a future gain, such as when you sell a rental property.

Year-end planning for your investments

If you sold an investment at a gain this year, you may be able to lower the tax by also selling one that has dropped in value. This is called tax-loss selling. The loss first reduces your capital gains for the same year.

Illustration only: you have a $6,000 gain from shares sold in June. Selling a fund that is down $4,000 cuts your net gain to $2,000, so only $1,000 is added to your income.

Watch the settlement date

A trade settles (the shares and cash actually change hands) a little after you sell. In Canada, most stock and ETF trades now settle one business day after the trade, which is called T+1. For a loss to count in 2026, the sale must settle by December 31, 2026.

  • For 2026, that generally makes Wednesday, December 30, 2026 the last day to trade, but confirm the cut-off with your broker. A December 31 sale settles in January 2027, so the loss would count for 2027.
  • Mutual funds and other investments may differ; ask your broker.

Don't trip the superficial loss rule

Buying the same investment back within 30 days after the sale, or buying it in the 30 days before and still owning it 30 days after, can wipe out the loss under the superficial loss rule above. This includes purchases by your spouse or partner, or a corporation either of you controls.

Registered accounts don't count

Losses inside an RRSP (registered retirement savings plan) can't be claimed on your return. Tax-loss selling works in a regular taxable (non-registered) account.

If your losses are bigger than your gains for 2026, the leftover net capital loss can be carried back to 2023, 2024 or 2025, or kept for future years. A sale should still make sense as an investment decision.

Checklist before you sell

  • Gather your purchase documents, including legal fees and commissions, to prove your ACB.
  • Keep receipts for improvements, such as a new roof or renovation, which add to your ACB. Repairs don't.
  • Note the date you bought and the date you'll sell. Under 365 days or 730 days can trigger the flipping rules.
  • Decide which years to designate as your principal residence, especially if your family owns more than one home.
  • Report the sale of your home on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND), even if no tax is owed.
  • Look up any net capital losses from past years.
  • Planning tax-loss selling? Place stock and ETF trades by December 30, 2026, and don't buy the same investment back within 30 days.
  • If you're moving abroad, read our leaving Canada guide first, because leaving Canada can affect the tax on property you still own.
