Capital gains in Canada: selling your home, a rental property or investments

Selling a home, a rental condo or some shares can bring a large profit. Here's how the CRA taxes that profit, when your home is tax-free, and the reporting steps that families and small landlords in Metro Vancouver often miss.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 2, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Only half of a capital gain is taxed; the proposed increase to two-thirds was cancelled in March 2025.
  • The gain is the sale price minus your adjusted cost base and selling costs.
  • The principal residence exemption can make your home's gain tax-free, but you must report the sale on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND).
  • Homes owned less than 365 days can be fully taxed as business income, and B.C. adds its own flipping tax for homes owned under 730 days.
  • Rental property gains are taxable, and past capital cost allowance can be added back to income.
  • Net capital losses can go back three years or forward with no time limit, against capital gains.

What a capital gain is, in plain words

When you sell something you own as an investment, such as shares, a mutual fund, a rental condo or a piece of land, the CRA calls it capital property. If you sell it for more than it cost you, the profit is a capital gain. If you sell it for less, it's a capital loss.

Three terms do most of the work:

  • Proceeds of disposition: what you get for the property. Usually this is simply the sale price.
  • Adjusted cost base (ACB): what the property cost you, plus the costs of buying it (such as commissions and legal fees), plus improvements that add lasting value. Normal repairs and upkeep don't count.
  • Outlays and expenses: the costs of selling, such as real estate commissions, legal fees, advertising and surveyor's fees.

The basic formula is:

Capital gain = proceeds − (ACB + outlays and expenses)

Illustration only: you bought shares for $10,000, including the trading fee. Years later you sell them for $16,000 and pay $100 in fees to sell. Your capital gain is $16,000 − ($10,000 + $100) = $5,900.

You report the sale on Schedule 3 of your tax return. The taxable part ends up on line 12700.

How much of the gain is taxed in 2026

You don't pay tax on the whole gain. Only part of it, called the taxable capital gain, is added to your income. The share that's taxed is called the inclusion rate.

With the planned increase cancelled (see below), the inclusion rate stays at one-half (50%). In the example above, half of the $5,900 gain, or $2,950, is added to your income and taxed along with your other income for the year.

The two-thirds increase is not happening. In 2024 the federal government proposed raising the inclusion rate to two-thirds, and in January 2025 it pushed the start date to January 1, 2026. On March 21, 2025, the Prime Minister announced that the increase was cancelled, and the CRA's own pages now note that the increase was cancelled. If you read older articles warning about a two-thirds rate, they are out of date.

A separate break, the lifetime capital gains exemption, can shelter gains on qualifying small business shares and farm or fishing property. The government has said it is keeping the increase of this limit to $1.25 million for qualifying sales on or after June 25, 2024. It doesn't apply to your home, a rental property or publicly traded shares.

Selling your home: the principal residence exemption

For most families, the home is their biggest asset. The principal residence exemption can make the gain on selling it fully tax-free. A principal residence can be a house, condo, apartment, cottage, mobile home or even a houseboat.

Which home qualifies

For each year, the home can count as your principal residence if:

  • you own it, alone or with someone else,
  • you, your spouse or common-law partner (or former one), or any of your children lived in it at some time in that year, and
  • you designate it, which means you formally name it as your principal residence for those years when you sell.

Since 1982, a family can designate only one home per year. "Family" here generally means you, your spouse or partner and your unmarried children under 18. The land that comes with the home is generally limited to half a hectare (about 1.24 acres).

You must report the sale, even if no tax is owed

Since the 2016 tax year, the CRA allows the exemption only if you report the sale and the designation on your return for the year you sell. You do this on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND). If the home was your principal residence for every year you owned it, or every year but one, you only fill in page 1 of the form.

Warning: if you forget, ask the CRA to fix that year's return as soon as you can. The CRA may accept a late designation, but the penalty can be $100 for each full month it's late, up to $8,000.

When only part of the gain is exempt

If the home wasn't your principal residence for every year you owned it, only part of the gain is tax-free. The exempt part is worked out like this:

Gain × (1 + years designated) ÷ years owned

The "plus one" protects you in the year you sell one home and buy another, since you can only designate one of them for that year. It only counts if you were a resident of Canada in the year you bought the home. This matters for newcomers who bought a home before moving here (see our newcomers' tax guide).

Illustration only: you owned a condo for 10 years and designate it for 7 of them. The exempt share is (1 + 7) ÷ 10, or 80%. On a $100,000 gain, $80,000 is tax-free and $20,000 is a capital gain, half of which is taxed.

A loss on selling your home can't be claimed.

Selling a rental property, suite or second home

A rental condo, an investment property or a second home you don't designate is taxed like other capital property: you pay tax on half of the gain.

  • Split land and building. When you sell a rental, you split the sale price between land and building and report each part separately.
  • Watch for recapture. If you claimed capital cost allowance (the tax version of depreciation) on the building, some or all of it can be added back to your income in the year of sale. This is fully taxable, not half. Our rental income guide explains how this works.
  • Part of your home rented? If you rented a suite and claimed capital cost allowance or made structural changes, part of your gain may be taxable. You split the price and ACB in a reasonable way, such as by floor area or number of rooms.
  • Moved out and rented the home? A change in use is treated as if you sold the home at its market value and bought it back. Special elections can delay this; see the rental guide.
  • Two properties, one designation. If your family owns a home and a cottage, you choose which one to designate for each year. Which one to choose depends on the numbers, so work it out before you file.

Selling soon after buying: federal and B.C. flipping rules

Two separate sets of rules target homes that are sold quickly. Both can apply to the same sale.

Federal residential property flipping rule

For sales on or after January 1, 2023, if you owned a home or rental property in Canada for less than 365 days in a row, any profit is treated as business income. That means it's fully taxable, not half, and the principal residence exemption isn't available. A loss on a flipped property is treated as zero.

The rule doesn't apply if you sold because of, or expecting, certain life events, including:

  • a death, or a new member joining your household (such as a birth or caring for an aging parent),
  • a separation, if you lived apart for at least 90 days before the sale,
  • a threat to your safety, or a serious illness or disability,
  • a job loss, or a move for work or school that brings you at least 40 km closer,
  • insolvency, or the home being destroyed or expropriated.

B.C. home flipping tax

Since January 1, 2025, B.C. has its own tax on profits from homes owned for less than 730 days. It's separate from income tax.

  • The rate is 20% of the taxable profit if you sell within 365 days of buying.
  • Over the following 365 days, the rate shrinks gradually, reaching zero at 730 days.
  • If you lived in the home as your primary residence and owned it for at least 365 days in a row, you may be able to deduct up to $20,000.
  • If the tax applies, you must file a separate B.C. return within 90 days of the sale.

Selling shares, funds and other investments

Gains on shares, ETFs and mutual funds held outside a registered plan follow the same formula. Your broker may send a T5008 slip or an account statement showing the sale, but you're still responsible for getting the ACB right.

  • Average your cost. If you bought the same shares or fund units at different times and prices, they are identical properties. Your ACB is the average cost per unit, recalculated each time you buy more, not the price of the batch you think you sold.
  • Superficial loss rule. If you sell at a loss and you, your spouse or partner, or a corporation either of you controls buys the same investment within 30 days before or after the sale, and still owns it 30 days after, you can't claim the loss. Instead, the loss is usually added to the ACB of the shares bought back, so you get the benefit later.
  • Foreign currency. Convert the sale price to Canadian dollars at the exchange rate on the sale date, and the cost at the rate when you bought.
  • Paid over time? If the buyer pays you over several years, you may be able to claim a reserve, which spreads the gain over up to five years.

Using capital losses

Half of a capital loss is an allowable capital loss. You first use it against your taxable capital gains in the same year. If losses are bigger than gains, the leftover is a net capital loss.

  • You can carry a net capital loss back three years to get back tax you paid on gains, using Form T1A. Don't file an amended return for this.
  • You can carry it forward with no time limit.
  • It can usually only reduce taxable capital gains, not your job or rental income.
  • Losses on personal-use property, such as your home, car or furniture, can't be claimed.

Tip: keep a record of unused net capital losses from past years. They can reduce or wipe out tax on a future gain, such as when you sell a rental property.

Checklist before you sell

  • Gather your purchase documents, including legal fees and commissions, to prove your ACB.
  • Keep receipts for improvements, such as a new roof or renovation, which add to your ACB. Repairs don't.
  • Keep the sale statement from your lawyer or notary showing the price, commission and legal fees.
  • Note the date you bought and the date you'll sell. Under 365 days or 730 days can trigger the flipping rules.
  • Decide which years to designate as your principal residence, especially if your family owns more than one home.
  • Report the sale of your home on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND), even if no tax is owed.
  • Check your ACB for shares and funds using average cost, and watch the 30-day window if you sell at a loss.
  • Look up any net capital losses from past years.
  • If you're moving abroad, read our leaving Canada guide first, because leaving Canada can affect the tax on property you still own.
