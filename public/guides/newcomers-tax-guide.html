New to Canada? Your first-year tax guide

Your first year in Canada brings a new tax system, new forms and new benefits. This guide walks you through what to do, from the day you arrive to your first tax return and beyond.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 25, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • For most newcomers, tax residency starts the day you settle in Canada with ties such as a home or family here.
  • Apply for benefits like the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit and Canada child benefit right away, without waiting for a tax return.
  • On your first return, report world income only from your residency date, and expect some credits to be reduced for the part year.
  • Property you own on arrival gets a new cost equal to its value that day, so keep proof of that value.
  • You don't file Form T1135 in your first year, but from the second year you must if your foreign assets cost more than $100,000.
  • TFSA room starts from your residency date ($7,000 for 2026), and RRSP room builds once you have Canadian earned income.

When you become a Canadian resident for tax

For tax, what matters is your residency, not your immigration status. Residency decides what Canada can tax. For most newcomers, tax residency starts on the first day you live in Canada.

The CRA (Canada Revenue Agency) looks at your residential ties, the links between your life and Canada:

  • Significant ties: a home in Canada, a spouse or common-law partner in Canada, and dependants (such as children) in Canada.
  • Secondary ties: personal property like a car or furniture, social ties such as membership in a local club or place of worship, economic ties such as a Canadian bank account or credit card, a Canadian driver's licence or passport, and provincial health insurance.

Someone who has no significant ties but stays in Canada for 183 days or more in a year may be a deemed resident, meaning the law treats them as a resident anyway. If you are unsure of your status, you can ask the CRA for its opinion using Form NR74, Determination of Residency Status (entering Canada).

Your SIN, or a temporary tax number

A Social Insurance Number (SIN) is a nine-digit number you need to work in Canada and to get government benefits. You apply through Service Canada.

If Service Canada can't give you a SIN, the CRA can issue a temporary tax number (TTN). You send your benefit application or tax return without a SIN and attach a letter explaining why you don't have one. To speed things up, you can include certified copies of identity documents, such as your passport, driver's licence or birth certificate.

Once you have a SIN, register for your CRA account (often called My Account). It shows your benefits, notices and savings limits.

Apply for benefits before your first tax return

You don't have to wait for your first tax return. You can apply as soon as you are a resident.

The benefits

  • Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (CGEB): a tax-free payment made four times a year to people with low and modest incomes. It replaced the GST/HST credit in July 2026, and the amount went up by 25% for five years. In 2026, an eligible single person can get up to $679 a year, and a couple with two children under 19 up to $1,358. Only one application is needed per household.
  • Canada child benefit (CCB): a tax-free monthly payment for children under 18 who live with you. For July 2026 to June 2027, the most you can get is $8,157 a year for each child under 6 and $6,883 for each child aged 6 to 17. You or your spouse must be a citizen, permanent resident, protected person (with a positive decision from the Immigration and Refugee Board), someone registered under the Indian Act, or a temporary resident. Temporary residents qualify from the 19th month, after living in Canada for the previous 18 months, if they hold a valid permit in that 19th month. Refugee claimants who hold only a Refugee Protection Claimant Document aren't eligible.
  • B.C. family benefit: once your child is registered for the CCB and you (and your spouse) file tax returns, you are signed up automatically. It is paid together with the CCB each month.

Reporting your income from before you arrived

Benefits are based on family income, so the CRA asks for your (and your spouse's) world income for up to two years before you became a resident. You report it on a Statement of Income (SOI), even if the amount was zero. Convert amounts to Canadian dollars using the Bank of Canada exchange rate on the date you received the income. You can send it online through your CRA account (it is processed right away), by mail or by phone.

Reporting this income doesn't mean Canada taxes it.

Documents not in English or French need a copy of the original and an acceptable translation.

Keep your payments going. If you are on a temporary permit, send the CRA your new permit before the current one expires, or your CCB payments will stop. If you applied to extend your permit and are still waiting for a decision, you are treated as keeping your status until IRCC decides.

Filing your first tax return

When it's due

Your first return covers the year you arrive. It is due April 30 of the next year, or June 15 if you are self-employed. For example, someone who arrived in 2025 filed by April 30, 2026. File every year after that, even with no income, to keep benefits flowing.

Your date of entry

On the return, you enter the date you became a resident for tax. Example: if you arrived and set up significant ties on June 8, you enter it as month and day, "0608".

What income to report

  • After you became a resident: your world income, from Canada and every other country, in Canadian dollars.
  • Before you became a resident: only certain Canadian income, such as pay for work done in Canada, business income from a business carried on in Canada, the taxable part of Canadian scholarships and grants, and taxable gains on taxable Canadian property.

Protected persons generally don't report money received from a charity (such as a church group) or an individual while they were non-residents, but wages from a charity that hired them are taxable.

Credits are reduced for a part year

A non-refundable tax credit lowers the tax you owe but can't create a refund on its own. Many of these, such as the basic personal amount (the income you can earn before paying federal tax), are cut in proportion to the days you were a resident. The formula is days resident divided by the days in the year. For 2026, the full federal basic personal amount is up to $16,452, so someone who became a resident on July 1, 2026 gets roughly half.

Some credits aren't cut by days. You claim them for amounts paid in the part of the year you were a resident. These include CPP and EI contributions, the Canada employment amount, tuition, medical expenses, donations, student loan interest and the home buyers' amount. For your months before arrival, you can claim the remaining credits in full only if your Canadian income was at least 90% of your net world income for that time.

Avoiding double tax

  • Foreign tax credit: if another country also taxed income you report in Canada, you may claim a federal foreign tax credit on Form T2209. See our foreign income and foreign tax credit guide.
  • Tax treaties: Canada has tax treaties with many countries that decide which country can tax which income. Income that a treaty exempts is still reported, then deducted on line 25600.

Other first-year points

  • You use the BC tax forms (Form 428 for B.C.) if you lived in BC on December 31.
  • Moving expenses to Canada generally aren't deductible.
  • Spousal or child support you pay may be deductible even if the recipient lives abroad.
  • Keep receipts and records for at least six years after you file.

Property you owned when you arrived

When you become a resident, the CRA treats most property you already own, such as shares, jewellery, paintings or collections, as if you sold it and bought it back at its fair market value (FMV) on the day your residency started. FMV is generally the highest price you could get for it in a normal sale on the open market.

That value becomes your cost base, the starting cost Canada uses to work out your gain. When you later sell or give away the property, Canada taxes only the gain after you arrived.

Example (made-up numbers): you bought shares abroad for $10,000. They were worth $30,000 when you arrived, and you sell them years later for $40,000. For Canadian tax, your gain starts from $30,000, so it is $10,000, not $30,000.

Get proof of value now. Save broker statements, bank balances and, for real estate, a valuation as of your arrival date. Finding these years later is hard.

Reporting foreign assets: Form T1135

Form T1135 tells the CRA about money and investments you hold outside Canada.

  • Not in your first year. You don't file T1135 for the tax year you first became a resident.
  • From the second year, you must file it if the total cost of your specified foreign property was more than $100,000 at any time in the year. For property you owned on arrival, the cost is its FMV on the day you became a resident.
  • What counts: money in foreign bank accounts, shares of foreign companies (even held through a Canadian broker), foreign property rented out for profit, and land that earns no income, such as a vacant plot.
  • What doesn't count: property used mainly for personal use (such as a holiday home you use yourself), personal items like jewellery and art, and investments inside your RRSP or TFSA.

Example: foreign shares that cost $75,000 plus $35,000 in a foreign bank account total $110,000, so T1135 is required, even if you cashed some out before December 31.

T1135 is due with your tax return. Penalties can apply for filing late or leaving things out.

TFSA, RRSP and FHSA for newcomers

Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA)

Your TFSA room starts building on the day you become a resident, if you are 18 or older. You get the yearly limit for the year you arrive, but no room for years before you lived here. For 2026, the limit is $7,000. The CRA hasn't announced the 2027 limit yet.

Putting in more than your room costs a tax of 1% a month on the extra, so check your room in your CRA account before you put in large amounts.

Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP)

RRSP room comes from earned income (mainly salary and self-employment income): generally 18% of the previous year's earned income, up to a yearly maximum of $33,810 for 2026 and $35,390 for 2027. Because the room is based on last year's income, a newcomer may have little or no room in the year they arrive. Your limit appears on your notice of assessment and in your CRA account.

A First Home Savings Account (FHSA) is open to residents 18 or older who are first-time home buyers: up to $8,000 a year, $40,000 in total. See our RRSP, TFSA and FHSA guide.

Notices of assessment and CRA scams

Keep every notice of assessment

After you file, the CRA sends a notice of assessment (NOA), a summary of your return and any refund or balance owing. It also shows your RRSP deduction limit. Save a PDF copy each year from your CRA account, since you may need it later.

Watch for scams

Fake "CRA" calls and texts often target newcomers. The real CRA will not:

  • threaten to deport or arrest you, or put you in prison;
  • demand payment right away by Interac e-transfer, gift cards, prepaid credit cards or cryptocurrency;
  • send refunds or benefits by e-transfer or text message;
  • ask for personal or financial information by email or voicemail.
If in doubt, hang up. Check your CRA account, or call the CRA using the number on canada.ca. Report scams to your local police and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.

Your first-year checklist

  1. Note your date of arrival and when you set up ties (home, family, bank account).
  2. Get your SIN (or a TTN) and register for your CRA account.
  3. Apply for benefits: RC151, or RC66 and RC66SCH if you have children.
  4. Report your world income for the two years before arrival (Statement of Income, or CTB9 for a non-resident spouse).
  5. Record the value of everything you owned on arrival, with proof.
  6. Keep records of foreign income and foreign tax paid after you arrived.
  7. File your first return by April 30 of the next year, with your entry date.
  8. From the second year, check whether your foreign assets cost more than $100,000 (T1135).
  9. Check TFSA and RRSP room in your CRA account before contributing.
  10. Save every notice of assessment, and ignore threatening "CRA" calls.

A newcomer's first return is one of the trickier ones to get right. If you're here on a study or work permit, see also our guide for international students and work permit holders.
