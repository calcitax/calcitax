Renting out a suite, condo or room: how rental income is taxed

Renting a basement suite, laneway home, condo or spare room is common across Metro Vancouver. Here's how the rent is taxed, which expenses you can deduct, how to protect the tax-free status of your home, and what changed in 2026.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 20, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Report rent and expenses on Form T776 with your return; co-owners each report their own share.
  • Repairs are deducted right away, but improvements are claimed over time through capital cost allowance (CCA).
  • If you live in the home, deduct only the rented share of whole-house costs, based on floor area or rooms.
  • Claiming CCA on part of your own home can make part of your gain taxable when you sell.
  • Non-compliant short-term rentals lose their deductions, and B.C. speculation and vacancy tax rates rise for 2026.
  • The federal Underused Housing Tax is gone for 2025 and later years; filing for 2022 to 2024 still applies.

Rental income or business income, and how to report it

Most landlords earn rental income. The CRA calls this "income from property". What decides it is how many services you give your tenants.

  • Rental income: you give the space plus basic services, such as heat, light, parking and laundry. This is the normal case for a basement suite, laneway home, condo or rented room.
  • Possibly business income: you also give extra services, such as cleaning, security or meals. The more you add, the more likely it is a business. A business is reported on Form T2125 instead. This guide covers ordinary rental income.

Form T776

You report rent and expenses on Form T776, Statement of Real Estate Rentals, which you file with your personal tax return. The CRA's guide T4036 explains each line.

  • Calendar year. Every rental property has a December 31 year-end, so you report rent from January 1 to December 31.
  • Accrual method. This means you count rent in the year you earn it and expenses in the year you owe them, even if the money moves later. You may use the simpler cash method (counting money when it is paid) only if the result would be almost the same.
  • Co-owners report their own share. If you and your spouse own the home 50/50, each of you reports half the rent and half the expenses. Owning a property together as an investment does not, on its own, make you a partnership.
  • Profit test. If you have no reasonable expectation of making a profit, you can't claim the expenses.

Renting to family for less

Many families rent a suite to a parent, adult child or sibling for less than they'd charge a stranger. The CRA sees this as sharing costs, not a profit-seeking rental, so you can't claim a rental loss. If you charge the normal rate and expect a profit, the usual rules apply. Small amounts a family member in your home chips in for groceries or upkeep are not rental income, and you can't claim expenses against them.

What you can deduct: current vs capital expenses

  • Current expenses keep the property running or put it back the way it was. You deduct them in full in the year.
  • Capital expenses give a lasting benefit or make the property better than it was. You can't deduct them all at once. Instead, you claim capital cost allowance over several years (explained below).
Current (deduct now)Capital (claim over time)
Repainting the outside of a wooden housePutting vinyl siding on the outside walls
Repairing worn wooden stepsReplacing wooden steps with concrete ones
Re-shingling a roof back to its original stateBuying a new fridge for the suite

Common costs you can deduct

  • Mortgage interest on money borrowed to buy or improve the rental (not the principal, which is the loan itself)
  • Property taxes for the time the unit was available to rent, and insurance. If you prepay insurance for several years, deduct one year at a time.
  • Repairs and maintenance
  • Utilities you pay under the rental arrangement, such as hydro, gas or water
  • Advertising, legal fees for leases or collecting rent, bookkeeping and accounting fees, and property management fees

What you can't deduct

  • Repayments of mortgage principal
  • The cost of land (it never wears out, so it is never depreciated)
  • The value of your own time and labour
  • Property transfer tax and legal fees for buying the property. These are added to the property's cost instead.
  • Interest on money you borrowed for personal use, even if the rental secures the loan

Renting part of the home you live in

If you live upstairs and rent the basement, or rent out a room, you can deduct:

  • 100% of costs that apply only to the rented part, such as advertising the suite or fixing its bathroom, and
  • a fair share of whole-house costs, such as property taxes, insurance, mortgage interest and shared utilities.

The CRA accepts splitting whole-house costs by floor area or by number of rooms.

Illustration (made-up numbers): your house is 2,400 square feet and the basement suite is 800, so one-third is rented. If your property tax is $4,500 and home insurance is $1,800 for the year, you could deduct $1,500 and $600 for the suite.

For a single room rented to a lodger, use the share of rooms rented, plus a fair share of rooms you both use, such as the kitchen.

Capital cost allowance (CCA) in plain language

Capital cost allowance (CCA) is the tax version of depreciation. It lets you deduct the cost of the building and items like appliances a little at a time, because they wear out over many years.

  • Classes and rates. Assets are grouped into classes, each with a yearly rate. Most rental buildings bought after 1987 are class 1 (4%). Furniture and household appliances are usually class 8 (20%).
  • Declining balance. The rate applies to what's left in the class each year, so the claim gets smaller over time.
  • Building only. Land can't be depreciated, so split the purchase price between land and building.
  • Separate class. Each rental property that cost $50,000 or more goes in its own class.
  • Half-year rule. In the year you buy, you can usually claim CCA on only half of the cost added.
  • Your choice. You can claim any amount from zero up to the maximum.
  • No losses. CCA can't create or increase a rental loss.

Bought a rental or new appliances in 2026? Rules that became law on March 26, 2026 bring back the accelerated investment incentive for many assets bought after 2024. Where it applies, it gives a bigger first-year claim and the half-year rule doesn't apply. The CRA's rental guide says this covers property put into use before 2034. Ask your preparer how it applies to your building and each appliance before you file your 2026 return.

When you sell: recapture and terminal loss

If you sell the building for more than its remaining undepreciated balance, the extra is added back to your income. This is called recapture. If you sell for less and nothing is left in the class, you can usually deduct the leftover balance as a terminal loss.

Why claiming CCA on your home can cost you later

For most Metro Vancouver homeowners, the biggest tax break is the principal residence exemption. It can make the gain on selling your home tax-free. Renting part or all of your home can affect it. Our guide to capital gains when you sell explains the exemption in more detail.

Renting part of your home

The CRA usually treats the whole home as still your principal residence if all three are true: the rental is small compared with your own use, you made no structural changes to rent it, and you claim no CCA on it.

If any one of these isn't true, the CRA treats the rented part as sold and bought back at market value when the rental started. This is called a change in use. Part of your gain on a later sale could then be taxable.

Tip: claiming CCA on part of your own home saves a little tax now but can cost far more when you sell. Many homeowners choose not to claim it. Talk it through before you do.

Turning your whole home into a rental

If you move out and rent your home, you're normally treated as selling it at market value on that date. You can make a subsection 45(2) election (a signed letter sent with that year's return) so no sale is treated as happening. You can then keep calling the home your principal residence for up to four years while it's rented, as long as you don't name another home for those years. You can't claim CCA while the election is in place.

Moving back into a former rental

If a rental becomes your home, a subsection 45(3) election can delay the deemed sale until you actually sell. You can't use it if CCA was claimed on the property for any year after 1984. Since March 19, 2019, both elections can also apply when only part of a property changes use.

Short-term rentals, GST and B.C. housing taxes

Short-term rentals

In B.C. communities where the rule applies, hosts may rent short-term only in their own principal residence (where they usually live), plus one secondary suite or accessory dwelling unit on the same property. Since May 1, 2025, every host must register with the provincial short-term rental registry and show the registration number on each listing.

Federal tax rules add a cost to breaking these rules. For income tax, a short-term rental is one rented, or offered for rent, for less than 90 days in a row. If the province or city doesn't allow it, or you don't meet a required registration, licence or permit, it is non-compliant. The CRA then denies the expenses and CCA for the non-compliant days.

GST/HST

Renting a home to someone who lives there for one month or more is generally exempt from GST/HST, so you don't charge it. Stays of less than a month can be taxable if you're registered for GST/HST. See our GST/HST and PST guide for when registration is needed.

B.C. speculation and vacancy tax

Owners of homes in the taxable areas must declare every year by March 31, even if nothing changed or they're exempt. Living in the home, or renting it out for six months or more, are common ways to qualify for an exemption. For declarations made in 2027 (covering 2026), the province is raising the rates to 1% for Canadian citizens and permanent residents with empty or underused homes (up from 0.5%) and 3% for foreign owners and owners with most of their income not reported in Canada (up from 2%).

Federal Underused Housing Tax

This federal tax mostly affected owners who aren't Canadian citizens or permanent residents. A law passed on March 26, 2026 means no tax is payable for 2025 and later years. Filing and tax rules for 2022 to 2024 still apply, including late-filing penalties.

If you move away and keep renting

Once you're a non-resident of Canada for tax purposes:

  • The person who pays you the rent, usually your property manager or other Canadian agent, must hold back 25% of the gross rent (rent before expenses) and send it to the CRA by the 15th of the next month.
  • You and your agent can file Form NR6 so the 25% is held back from net rent (after expenses) instead. It's due by January 1 each year, or before the first rent payment if that's earlier.
  • You can file a section 216 return (Form T1159) to pay tax on your net rental income. It's generally due within two years after the end of the year. If you had an approved NR6, the deadline is June 30 of the next year (for example, June 30, 2026 for 2025).

Our leaving Canada guide covers the move itself.

What's new for landlords in late 2026

  • Federal Budget 2026 was tabled on November 5, 2026. Two of its proposals touch landlords:
  • Proposed: the federal budget would move the personal tax filing and payment deadline to May 15, starting with the 2027 tax year. This is not law yet, and it doesn't change the deadlines for your 2026 return.
  • Proposed: for non-residents selling Canadian property on or after January 1, 2027, the amount a buyer must hold back on most sales would drop from 25% to 15% of the price. This is also not law yet.

Year-end tip: before December 31, gather your 2026 rent records, receipts and a note of anything you bought for the rental this year, such as appliances or flooring.

Checklist and common mistakes

Records checklist

  • Leases, a rent ledger and bank deposit records
  • Receipts and invoices for every expense
  • Purchase papers showing the split between land and building
  • Your floor-area or room calculation, if you live in the home
  • Your CCA history, year by year
  • Your short-term rental registration and any city licence, if you host short stays

Keep records for six years from the end of the tax year. Don't send them with your return, but have them ready if the CRA asks. Our record-keeping guide has more tips.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Not reporting rent from a suite or room because it's paid in cash or by a relative.
  • Deducting capital costs, such as a new kitchen or appliances, as repairs.
  • Deducting mortgage principal, or claiming 100% of whole-house costs when you live there too.
  • Claiming a loss on a below-market rental to family.
  • Claiming CCA on your own home without weighing the effect on the principal residence exemption.
  • Running a non-compliant short-term rental, which costs you your deductions for those days.
  • Missing the March 31 speculation and vacancy tax declaration, even when you're exempt.
  • Forgetting the 25% withholding after moving abroad.
