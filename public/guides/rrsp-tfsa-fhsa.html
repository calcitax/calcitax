RRSP vs TFSA vs FHSA: which account is right for you?

Canada has three main tax-advantaged accounts for saving. Each one works differently, and choosing the right one, or the right mix, can save you thousands. Here's how they compare, in plain language.

The short version

TFSA

Flexible, any goal

No tax deduction when you put money in, but all growth and withdrawals are tax-free. Take money out any time, for anything.

RRSP

Retirement

Contributions lower your taxes today. Money grows tax-deferred, and you pay tax when you take it out, ideally in retirement when your income is lower.

FHSA

Your first home

The best of both: contributions are tax-deductible and withdrawals for your first home are tax-free. Only for first-time buyers.

In all three accounts you can hold the same kinds of investments: savings deposits, GICs, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs and stocks. The account is just the tax "wrapper" around them.

TFSA: Tax-Free Savings Account

How it works

You contribute money you've already paid tax on. Everything it earns, whether interest, dividends or capital gains, is never taxed, and you can take money out at any time without paying tax.

Who can open one

  • You must be a Canadian resident, at least 18 years old, with a valid Social Insurance Number.
  • You start building room in the year you turn 18. Newcomers start building room from the year they become residents (if 18 or older). Room isn't backdated for years before you lived in Canada.

Limits

  • 2026 limit: $7,000. The CRA hasn't announced the 2027 limit yet; it's usually published in November.
  • Total room: someone who was 18 or older and living in Canada every year since 2009, and has never contributed, has $109,000 of room in 2026.
  • Unused room carries forward forever. Anything you withdraw is added back to your room on January 1 of the following year.

Pros

  • All growth and withdrawals are tax-free
  • Withdraw any time, for any reason
  • Withdrawals don't reduce income-tested benefits like the Canada Child Benefit, GST/HST credit or Old Age Security
  • No age limit and no need to have earned income
  • Withdrawn amounts can be re-contributed later

Cons

  • No tax deduction for contributions
  • Easy access can tempt you to spend your savings
  • Re-contributing in the same year you withdrew can trigger an over-contribution penalty
  • Contributions made while you're a non-resident are penalized

RRSP: Registered Retirement Savings Plan

How it works

Your contributions are deducted from your income, which lowers the tax you pay this year. The money grows without tax until you withdraw it, and then the withdrawal is taxed as income. The idea is to get the deduction while you're earning a higher income and pay the tax later, in retirement, when your income and tax rate are usually lower.

Who can contribute

  • Anyone with RRSP room, which comes from earned income (mainly employment and self-employment income) reported on a tax return.
  • You can contribute to your own RRSP until December 31 of the year you turn 71. You can still contribute to a spousal RRSP after that, until the end of the year your spouse or common-law partner turns 71.

Limits

  • Your new room each year is 18% of the previous year's earned income, up to an annual maximum: $33,810 for 2026 and $35,390 for 2027. If you're in a workplace pension plan, your room is reduced by your pension adjustment.
  • Unused room carries forward. Your exact limit is printed on your Notice of Assessment and shown in your CRA account.
  • Contributions made in the first 60 days of the year can be deducted on the previous year's return. For the 2026 tax year, the deadline is March 1, 2027.
  • You can over-contribute up to $2,000 without penalty. Beyond that, the excess is taxed at 1% per month.

Withdrawals

Withdrawals are added to your income, and your bank withholds tax at the source: 10% on amounts up to $5,000, 20% on $5,000 to $15,000, and 30% over $15,000 (outside Quebec). The withholding often isn't the full tax, so you may owe more at tax time. Unlike a TFSA, the room you used is gone for good.

By the end of the year you turn 71, you must convert your RRSP to a RRIF (Registered Retirement Income Fund), buy an annuity, or withdraw it as cash.

Spousal RRSP

You can contribute to your spouse's or partner's RRSP using your own room and claim the deduction yourself. In retirement, the money is taxed in their hands, which can split income between you. If they withdraw it within about three years of your contributions, the withdrawal may be taxed back to you.

Pros

  • Contributions reduce your taxes now, often creating a refund
  • Growth is tax-deferred for decades
  • Higher limits than a TFSA for higher earners
  • Spousal RRSPs help couples split retirement income
  • Can be used for your first home (HBP) or education (LLP)

Cons

  • Withdrawals are fully taxable, with tax withheld up front
  • Withdrawals can reduce income-tested benefits such as OAS and the GST/HST credit
  • Room used on a withdrawal is lost
  • Less useful when your income is low and you pay little tax
  • Must be converted by the end of the year you turn 71

RRSP programs for homes and school

  • Home Buyers' Plan (HBP): first-time buyers can withdraw up to $60,000 from their RRSPs tax-free to buy or build a home, and repay it over 15 years. Normally repayments start in the second year after the withdrawal. For a first withdrawal made between January 1, 2026 and December 31, 2028, repayments are deferred to start in the fifth year (for example, a 2026 withdrawal starts repayments in 2031). Missed repayments are added to your income.
  • Lifelong Learning Plan (LLP): withdraw up to $10,000 a year, and $20,000 in total, to pay for full-time education or training for you or your spouse, then repay over 10 years.

FHSA: First Home Savings Account

How it works

The FHSA combines the best parts of the RRSP and TFSA. Contributions are tax-deductible like an RRSP, and withdrawals to buy your first home are tax-free like a TFSA.

Who can open one

  • You must be a Canadian resident, at least 18 (19 in provinces where that's the age of majority, including BC), and no older than 71 at the end of the year you open it.
  • You must be a first-time home buyer: in the current year and the four previous calendar years, you didn't live in a home you owned or jointly owned. If you have a spouse or common-law partner, the same applies to a home they owned that you lived in.

Limits

  • $8,000 a year, up to $40,000 in your lifetime.
  • Room only starts once you open the account. Up to $8,000 of unused room carries forward, so the most you can contribute in one year is $16,000.
  • Unlike the RRSP, contributions must be made by December 31 to count for that year.
  • You don't have to deduct your contributions right away. You can carry the deduction forward to a year when your income, and the tax saving, is higher.

Withdrawals

A withdrawal is tax-free if it's a qualifying withdrawal: you're still a first-time buyer, you have a written agreement to buy or build a qualifying home in Canada before October 1 of the following year, and you plan to live in it as your main home within one year.

The account must be closed by December 31 of the earliest of: the 15th anniversary of opening your first FHSA, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. If you don't buy a home, you can transfer the money to your RRSP tax-free without using any RRSP room, or withdraw it as taxable income.

Pros

  • Tax deduction going in and tax-free coming out for a home
  • Unlike the HBP, nothing has to be paid back
  • Can be combined with the Home Buyers' Plan for the same home
  • If plans change, it rolls into your RRSP without using RRSP room
  • The deduction can be saved for a higher-income year

Cons

  • Only for first-time buyers
  • Room doesn't build until you open the account, so open it early
  • Lower limits: $8,000 a year and $40,000 for life
  • Non-qualifying withdrawals are fully taxable
  • Must be closed within about 15 years

Buying your first home: using all three

First-time buyers can combine accounts for the same home. Per person, that's up to:

  • $40,000 of FHSA contributions plus any growth, withdrawn tax-free and never repaid
  • $60,000 from RRSPs through the Home Buyers' Plan, repaid over 15 years
  • Whatever you've saved in your TFSA, withdrawn tax-free

A couple who are both first-time buyers can each do this, which can add up to $200,000 or more toward a down payment before counting TFSAs.

Which should you use first?

There's no single right answer, but these are common starting points:

Planning to buy your first home

Open an FHSA now, even with a small deposit, so your room starts building. Max it out before anything else, then use your RRSP (with the HBP) and TFSA.

Lower income (roughly under $55,000)

Usually TFSA first. Your RRSP deduction would save little tax, and RRSP withdrawals later could reduce benefits. Save the RRSP room for when your income rises.

Higher income

The RRSP often comes first, because the deduction saves tax at a higher rate. Put your refund into your TFSA to make it work twice.

Building an emergency fund

A TFSA, since you can take money out at any time without tax and get the room back next year.

Self-employed or variable income

Use the TFSA in lower-income years and the RRSP in higher-income years to get the most from each deduction.

New to Canada

Your TFSA room starts the year you arrive (if 18 or older), and RRSP room starts once you have Canadian earned income and file a return. Check whether you qualify as a first-time buyer for an FHSA, and remember to report any foreign property over $100,000.

The income levels above are rough guides. The best mix depends on your tax bracket now and in retirement, your goals and your benefits.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Over-contributing to a TFSA by withdrawing and putting money back in the same year. The room only comes back on January 1.
  • Guessing your limits. Check your CRA account or Notice of Assessment. Bank records don't show contributions you made at other institutions.
  • Waiting to open an FHSA. No room builds until the account exists.
  • Leaving cash uninvested. A registered account holding cash earning nothing wastes its tax advantage. Choose investments that suit your timeline and risk.
  • Missing HBP repayments, which are added to your taxable income.
  • Contributing while living abroad to a TFSA, which is penalized every month.
  • US citizens: TFSAs and FHSAs may not be tax-sheltered under US tax rules. Get advice before contributing.
