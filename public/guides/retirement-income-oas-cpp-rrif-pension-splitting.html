Retirement income in Canada: OAS, CPP, RRIFs and pension splitting

Old Age Security, the Canada Pension Plan and your RRSP or RRIF savings each follow their own rules on timing and tax. Here's how they work in 2026, how to avoid the OAS clawback, what you must do with your RRSP at 71, and how couples can split pension income to pay less tax.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 4, 2026 · Figures for 2026 (OAS and GIS: October to December 2026)

In short

  • The maximum OAS pension for October to December 2026 is $762.50 a month at 65 to 74 and $838.75 at 75 and older.
  • For 2026, OAS starts to be clawed back at 15% of net income over $95,323.
  • You can start CPP between 60 and 70: it drops 0.6% a month before 65 and rises 0.7% a month after 65.
  • By the end of the year you turn 71, you must turn your RRSP into a RRIF, an annuity or cash, and RRIFs have a yearly minimum withdrawal.
  • Couples can move up to 50% of eligible pension income to each other using Form T1032, but CPP and OAS can't be split this way.

The main pieces of retirement income

Most retirees live on a mix of government pensions and their own savings. Each piece has its own rules.

  • Old Age Security (OAS): a monthly federal pension for people 65 and older. It is based on how long you have lived in Canada, not on your work history.
  • Guaranteed Income Supplement (GIS): an extra monthly payment on top of OAS for seniors with low income.
  • Canada Pension Plan (CPP): a monthly pension based on what you paid into CPP from your work pay over the years.
  • Registered retirement income fund (RRIF): an account that pays your own savings back to you as income, usually after you move money from your registered retirement savings plan (RRSP) into it.
  • Workplace pensions: monthly payments from a pension plan you belonged to at work.

OAS, CPP, RRIF payments and workplace pensions are all taxable. That means they count as income on your tax return. GIS is not taxable. This guide uses figures for 2026, and for OAS and GIS, the October to December 2026 quarter.

Old Age Security and the Guaranteed Income Supplement

Who can get OAS

To get OAS while living in Canada, you must be 65 or older, a Canadian citizen or legal resident when your application is approved, and have lived in Canada for at least 10 years since turning 18. If you live outside Canada, you need at least 20 years of residence in Canada after age 18.

A full pension is based on 40 years in Canada after age 18. With fewer years, you get a partial pension: your years in Canada divided by 40. So 20 years in Canada after 18 gives about half.

How much you can get (October to December 2026)

PaymentMaximum per month
OAS, age 65 to 74$762.50
OAS, age 75 and older$838.75
GIS, single person (yearly income under $23,112, not counting OAS)$1,138.90

The higher amount at 75 comes from a permanent 10% increase that starts the month after your 75th birthday. OAS amounts are reviewed every January, April, July and October to keep up with the cost of living. They went up 1.4% for October to December 2026, and they don't go down when prices fall.

Delaying OAS

You can put off your first OAS payment for up to 5 years after 65. For each month you wait, your pension goes up by 0.6%, up to 36% more if you start at 70.

GIS and the B.C. Senior's Supplement

GIS amounts depend on your income and your spouse's income. Couples have different limits from single people. GIS is recalculated every July using your net income from the year before, so filing your tax return on time matters.

In B.C., low-income seniors who get OAS and GIS may also get the Senior's Supplement from the province: up to $99.30 a month for a single senior and up to $220.50 a month for a couple. You don't need to apply; it is paid automatically to people who qualify.

The OAS clawback (recovery tax)

If your income is high, you may have to pay back some or all of your OAS. The official name is the OAS pension recovery tax, but most people call it the clawback.

  • For 2026, the clawback starts when your individual net income is over $95,323. (For 2025 income, it was $93,454.)
  • You repay 15% of your income above that threshold.
  • Based on Service Canada's figures for October to December 2026, your OAS is fully clawed back at about $155,320 of income if you are 65 to 74, and about $161,320 if you are 75 or older.

Net income is the amount on line 23600 of your tax return: your total income after deductions such as RRSP contributions. It includes your OAS, CPP, pensions, RRIF withdrawals and investment income.

Illustration only: if your 2026 net income ends up $10,000 over the threshold, you would repay 15% of $10,000, or $1,500.

The repayment is taken out of future OAS payments. For example, the amount based on 2025 income comes off payments from July 2026 to June 2027, spread over 12 months.

Tip: Because the clawback looks at your own net income, not your family's, pension income splitting (explained below) can sometimes lower or remove it. A large one-time RRIF or RRSP withdrawal can do the opposite and push you over the line for that year.

The Canada Pension Plan: when to start

You can start your CPP retirement pension any time from age 60 to age 70. The age you pick changes your monthly amount for life:

If you startChange to your monthly pension
Before 65Goes down 0.6% for each month before 65 (up to 36% less at 60)
At 65Your standard amount
After 65Goes up 0.7% for each month after 65 (up to 42% more at 70)

There is no gain from waiting past 70, because the increase stops then.

For a pension starting at 65 in January 2026, the most anyone could get is $1,507.65 a month. Most people get less, because it depends on how much and how long you contributed. The average for new retirees starting at 65 was $858.34 a month in July 2026. Your My Service Canada Account shows an estimate based on your own record.

Starting early means more years of smaller payments; starting late means fewer years of bigger ones. Your health, savings and work plans all matter, so there is no single right answer.

CPP pension sharing

CPP has its own way to share income with a spouse or common-law partner, called pension sharing. If you live together and at least one of you gets (or has applied for) a CPP retirement pension, you can ask Service Canada to split it between you. This can lower your combined tax. You apply through My Service Canada Account or on form ISP1002. It is separate from the tax-return pension splitting covered below, because CPP cannot be split on your tax return.

CPP payments are reported on your tax return from your T4A(P) slip.

From RRSP to RRIF: the age-71 deadline and minimum withdrawals

December 31 of the year you turn 71 is the last day you can put money into your own RRSP. In that year, you must choose what to do with your RRSP savings:

  • cash it out (the whole amount is taxed as income that year, which can be very costly);
  • move it to a RRIF; or
  • buy an annuity, a contract that pays you a set income, often for life.

No tax is taken off when money moves straight into a RRIF or is used to buy an annuity.

Minimum withdrawals

A RRIF must pay you at least a minimum amount every year, starting the year after you open it. The minimum for the first year is zero. Each year, the minimum is the RRIF's value on January 1 multiplied by a percentage set by your age at the start of the year:

  • Under 71: 1 divided by (90 minus your age). At 65, that is 1/25, or 4%.
  • 71 and older: a fixed percentage from the CRA's table, for example 5.28% at 71, 5.82% at 75, 6.82% at 80, 8.51% at 85, 11.92% at 90 and 20% at 95 or older.

Illustration only: a RRIF worth $200,000 on January 1, for someone who is 71 at that date, must pay out at least $200,000 × 5.28% = $10,560 that year.

If your spouse or common-law partner is younger, you can choose to base the minimum on their age, which gives a lower minimum. You must make this choice before you receive any payments from that RRIF, and you can't change it later.

Watch the tax: Your RRIF carrier doesn't withhold tax on the minimum amount, but it must withhold tax on anything you take above it. Withholding is only a prepayment. You may still owe tax in April. Talk to your carrier or accountant early so a tax bill doesn't catch you off guard. For comparison, a cash withdrawal from an RRSP has 10% withheld on amounts up to $5,000, 20% from $5,000 to $15,000 and 30% over $15,000 (outside Quebec), and that is often less than the tax you really owe.

Pension income splitting, the pension income amount and the age amount

Pension income splitting

Canada taxes each person on their own income, and tax rates rise as income goes up. So a couple usually pays less tax when their incomes are close together. Pension income splitting lets you move up to 50% of your eligible pension income to your spouse or common-law partner on your tax returns. No money actually changes hands; it is only a tax calculation.

What counts as eligible pension income depends on the age of the person transferring it:

  • Any age: the taxable part of lifetime payments from a workplace pension plan.
  • 65 or older at the end of the year: also RRIF payments (including payments from a life income fund, or LIF, which is a special kind of RRIF) and annuity payments from an RRSP.
  • Never eligible: OAS, CPP, and U.S. individual retirement account (IRA) income.

To split, you and your partner both fill out and sign Form T1032, Joint Election to Split Pension Income, and each of you files it with your own return by the filing deadline. The information on both forms must match. Any tax withheld on the pension moves with it in the same proportion. You can choose a different percentage each year.

You can't split if, at the end of the year, you had been living apart because your relationship broke down for 90 days or more. Both of you must also be Canadian residents on December 31. If you forgot to split in an earlier year, you can generally ask the CRA to accept a late election within three years after that year's filing due date.

Illustration only: Asha, 68, has a $40,000 workplace pension. Her husband has $10,000 of income. Asha could move up to $20,000 to him. Moving $15,000 would leave them with about $25,000 each, which often means less tax in total.

The pension income amount

The pension income amount is a federal tax credit on up to $2,000 of eligible pension income a year (the CRA's figure for 2025). If one spouse has no eligible pension income, splitting even a small amount can let that spouse claim this credit too.

The age amount

If you are 65 or older on December 31, you can claim the age amount, a federal tax credit of up to $9,208 for 2026. It starts to shrink once your net income is over $46,432 (2026). If you can't use all of it, you may be able to transfer some to your spouse or common-law partner. Splitting changes each spouse's net income, so it can also change how much of the age amount each of you keeps. Our guide to often-missed tax credits covers other credits seniors can claim.

Checklist and common mistakes

Checklist

  • Checked your CPP estimate in My Service Canada Account and thought about when to start (60 to 70).
  • Applied for OAS, or decided to delay it, around age 65.
  • If your income is low, made sure you are getting GIS (and the B.C. Senior's Supplement follows automatically).
  • Planned what to do with your RRSP before December 31 of the year you turn 71.
  • If your spouse is younger, considered using their age for your RRIF minimum before the first payment.
  • Arranged enough tax withholding on RRIF and pension payments to avoid a surprise bill.
  • Filed Form T1032 with both returns if splitting pension income.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to split CPP or OAS on the tax return. CPP uses Service Canada's separate pension sharing instead, and OAS can't be split.
  • Splitting RRIF income when the person transferring it is under 65.
  • Taking a big RRIF or RRSP withdrawal in one year and triggering the OAS clawback.
  • Assuming the tax withheld on RRIF payments covers all the tax owed.
  • Forgetting about foreign pensions. A pension from India or another country usually has to be reported on your Canadian return. If you are thinking of moving abroad in retirement, see our leaving Canada tax guide.
