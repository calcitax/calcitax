Sole proprietor or corporation? Choosing a structure in BC

Most small businesses in BC start as sole proprietorships, and many later wonder whether to incorporate. Here's how the two structures compare on cost, tax, liability and paperwork, so you can weigh the factors for your own business.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 2, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • A sole proprietor is taxed on all business profit personally, on Form T2125; a corporation files its own T2 return and pays its own tax.
  • A small Canadian-controlled private corporation pays about 11% combined federal and BC tax on its first $500,000 of active business income.
  • The lower rate mainly defers tax on profit left in the company; you pay personal tax when you take it out.
  • Salary builds CPP and RRSP room; dividends don't, but they're taxed with a dividend tax credit.
  • Limited liability doesn't cover personal guarantees or unremitted payroll deductions.
  • A corporation costs more to set up and run: BC fees, a T2 return, an annual report and more formal bookkeeping.

Sole proprietorship or corporation: what's the difference?

A sole proprietorship is the simplest way to run a business. You and the business are the same person in the eyes of the law. You keep all the profit, you make all the decisions, and you carry all the risk. If the business owes money, the CRA notes that the risk can reach your personal property and assets.

A corporation (a company) is a separate legal "person." It can own property, sign contracts, borrow money and pay tax in its own name. You own it through shares, so you're a shareholder, and you usually act as its director (the person who manages it) too. The company keeps existing even if the shares change hands.

Neither one is "better." Many businesses start as sole proprietorships and incorporate later, once profits grow. The right choice depends on how much you earn, how much you need to live on, the risks in your line of work, and how much paperwork you're ready to take on.

Setting up in BC: registration, names and costs

Sole proprietorship

  • If you do business under your own legal name (for example, "Priya Sharma"), you don't need to register the business or request a name with the province.
  • If you use any other name (for example, "Fraser Valley Cleaning"), you must first get the name approved and then register the business with BC Registries.
  • Government fees: $30 for a name request ($100 for priority service), then $40 to register online through the BC Business Registry or in person at Service BC. Register within 56 days of the name being approved, or the approval expires.

BC corporation

  • Reserve a name for $30 (it must end with a word like Inc. or Ltd.), or skip the name and use a numbered company such as "0123456 B.C. Ltd."
  • Prepare the company's articles (its rule book) and apply to incorporate. The provincial fee for most companies is $350.
  • Every year, file a BC annual report within two months of your incorporation anniversary. The fee is $43.39. Missing it two years in a row can lead to the company being dissolved.

Federal corporation

  • Incorporating federally with Corporations Canada costs $200 online and protects your name across Canada. The federal annual return is $12 online.
  • If you operate in BC, a federal company must also register in BC as an "extraprovincial" company ($350) and file BC annual reports as well.

These are government filing fees only. Legal help with articles and share structure, accounting set-up and a municipal business licence (from Surrey, Vancouver or your own city) are separate costs.

How each one is taxed

Sole proprietor: on your personal return

You report the business's income and expenses on Form T2125 and include the profit on your personal T1 return. It's added to any other income you have and taxed at your personal rates. For 2026, the combined federal and BC rate on each extra dollar is 28.2% once your taxable income is between $58,523 and $100,728, and it climbs from there. A business loss can reduce your other income, such as a part-time job, in the same year.

Corporation: its own return and its own rates

The company files a T2 corporate return every year, within six months of its year-end, even if it owes no tax. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) earning active business income can claim the small business deduction, which lowers the tax rate on its first $500,000 of active business income each year (the "business limit").

  • Federal rate with the small business deduction: 9%.
  • BC small business rate: 2%.
  • Combined: about 11% on income up to the $500,000 limit, compared with 27% (15% federal plus 12% BC) on income above it.

The $500,000 limit is shared between associated companies (for example, two companies owned by the same person) and shrinks if the company's investment income goes over $50,000 a year. Losses stay inside the company: you can't use them on your personal return.

The "tax deferral" idea

Here's a simple illustration with round numbers. Say your business makes $150,000 of profit and you need $90,000 a year to live on.

  • As a sole proprietor, all $150,000 is taxed on your personal return this year. The last $60,000 is taxed at combined 2026 rates that range from 28.2% up to 40.7% as your income rises.
  • With a corporation, you could pay yourself $90,000 and leave the other $60,000 in the company. At about 11%, the company pays roughly $6,600 of tax on it, leaving about $53,400 to reinvest in the business or save.
Deferral isn't the same as saving. When you later take that money out as a dividend, you pay personal tax on it. The benefit comes mainly from timing: it helps most when you don't need all the profit for living costs. If you take out everything the business earns each year, incorporating may save little or nothing once extra costs are counted.

Paying yourself from a corporation: salary or dividends

A sole proprietor simply takes money out of the business. Those "drawings" aren't a deduction, and you're taxed on the profit whether you take it out or not. With a corporation, the money belongs to the company, so you need a proper way to pay yourself. The two main options:

Salary (or bonus)

  • The company runs payroll, deducts tax, and issues you a T4 slip.
  • The company deducts CPP contributions from your pay and also pays a matching employer share, so both halves of CPP are paid, which builds your future CPP pension.
  • Salary counts as earned income, so it creates RRSP room (18% of the previous year's earned income, up to the yearly maximum).
  • Salary is an expense for the company, which lowers its taxable income.

Dividends

  • The company pays dividends out of its after-tax profit and reports them on a T5 slip.
  • Dividends aren't employment earnings, so no CPP is paid on them. That saves cash now, but it also means no CPP pension built on that income.
  • Dividends don't count as earned income, so they create no RRSP room.
  • On your personal return, dividends are "grossed up" and then reduced by the dividend tax credit. There are two kinds, "eligible" and "other than eligible" dividends, with different gross-ups (38% and 15%) and credits.

Many owners use a mix of both. The best mix depends on your income needs, retirement plans, RRSP and CPP goals, and whether family members work in the business. This is an area where a tax professional can help you run the numbers each year.

Liability protection and the extra work of a corporation

How much protection does a company really give?

In general, shareholders aren't responsible for a corporation's debts, so a supplier or customer with a claim against the business usually can't come after your house. But there are important limits:

  • Personal guarantees. Banks and landlords often ask a small company's owner to personally guarantee a loan or lease. If you sign, you owe that debt if the company can't pay.
  • Directors' liability. Directors can be personally liable for payroll deductions, CPP and other amounts the company was required to withhold or remit but didn't.

Extra costs and filings

  • A T2 return every year, with financial statements, due six months after year-end. Any tax owing is usually due three months after year-end for a CCPC claiming the small business deduction (two months otherwise).
  • The BC annual report (and the federal annual return if federally incorporated).
  • A separate business bank account and proper bookkeeping, since the company's money isn't yours.
  • Payroll filings and T4 slips if you pay salary, and T5 slips if you pay dividends.
  • Minute book and company records kept at the registered office.
  • Higher accounting fees than for a simple T2125.

Selling your business one day

Owners who sell shares of a qualifying small business corporation may be able to shelter a large capital gain with the lifetime capital gains exemption. The CRA's guidance for 2025 shows a limit of $1,250,000, describes that figure as a proposed change, and says the limit is indexed to inflation. The conditions are strict and need planning years ahead. A sole proprietor selling a business can't use this exemption on shares, because there are no shares to sell.

GST/HST applies either way

Whether you're a sole proprietor or a corporation, you must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales (together with associated businesses) go over $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters. Taxi and ride-share drivers must register no matter how much they earn. Incorporating doesn't change this.

Signs it may be time to incorporate

There's no single income level where incorporating becomes right. These are the factors to weigh:

  • You're earning more than you spend. If profit is well above what you need to live on, and you'd leave the extra in the business, the lower corporate rate can help you grow faster.
  • Your business carries real risk. Contracts, employees, a lease, or customers who could sue may make limited liability worth having, keeping in mind its limits.
  • You need to raise money. A corporation can usually raise larger amounts of capital, for example by selling shares to an investor.
  • You might sell one day. Selling shares of a qualifying company may let you use the lifetime capital gains exemption.
  • Family members are involved. Paying a spouse or adult child, or making them shareholders, adds options but also special tax rules, so get advice before you set things up.

On the other side, staying a sole proprietor often makes sense when you're just starting, expect losses in the early years (which can reduce your other income), need all the profit to live on, or want to keep paperwork and costs low.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing personal and business money. Paying groceries from the company account, or company bills from your own card, makes bookkeeping messy and can create tax problems. Use a separate account and card.
  • Treating company money as your own. Money you take out of a corporation that isn't salary, a dividend or a repaid expense becomes a shareholder loan. If it's not repaid within one year after the end of the company's tax year (and isn't part of a pattern of borrowing and repaying), it can be added to your personal income.
  • Incorporating only for the "11% rate". If you take out every dollar each year, the deferral advantage mostly disappears, while the extra costs remain.
  • Forgetting the annual report. Two missed years can get the company dissolved.
  • Not registering a business name. In BC, a sole proprietor using any name other than their own legal name must get it approved and register it.
  • Assuming a company protects everything. Personal guarantees and unpaid payroll remittances can still land on you.
  • Ignoring GST/HST. The $30,000 threshold applies either way, and ride-share drivers must register from day one.
