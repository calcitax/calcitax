Sole proprietor or corporation? Choosing a structure in BC

Most small businesses in BC start as sole proprietorships, and many later wonder whether to incorporate. Here's how the two structures compare on cost, tax, CPP, liability and paperwork, plus what the 2026 federal budget proposed, so you can weigh the factors for your own business.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 23, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • A sole proprietor reports business profit on Form T2125 with their T1 return; a corporation files its own T2 return every year, even with no tax owing.
  • In 2026, a small Canadian-controlled private corporation pays about 11% combined federal and BC tax on its first $500,000 of active business income, which mainly defers tax on profit left in the company.
  • Salary builds CPP and RRSP room; dividends don't, but they're taxed with a dividend tax credit.
  • Limited liability doesn't cover personal guarantees or payroll and GST/HST amounts a company failed to remit.
  • A corporation costs more to set up and run: BC fees, a T2 return, an annual report and more formal bookkeeping.
  • Budget 2026 proposed a single May 15 filing date from the 2027 tax year and a $7,500 instalment threshold from 2028. These are not law yet.

Sole proprietorship or corporation: what's the difference?

A sole proprietorship is the simplest way to run a business. In law, you and the business are the same person. You keep all the profit, make all the decisions and carry all the risk. The CRA points out that this risk can reach your personal property and assets.

A corporation (a company) is a separate legal "person." It can own property, sign contracts, borrow money and pay tax in its own name. You own it through shares, which makes you a shareholder. Most small-business owners are also the company's director, the person who manages it.

Neither one is "better." Many businesses start as sole proprietorships and incorporate once profits grow. The right choice depends on your income, your living costs, your risks and how much paperwork you can handle.

Setting up in BC: registration, names and costs

Sole proprietorship

  • If you run the business under your own legal name (for example, "Priya Sharma"), you don't have to register it with the province.
  • If you use any other name (for example, "Fraser Valley Cleaning"), you first get the name approved through a name request, then register the business with BC Registries online or at a Service BC office.
  • Government fees: $30 for a name request, then $40 to register. An approved name is held for 56 days, so register within that time.

BC corporation

  • Reserve a name for $30 ($130 for priority service). A company name needs an ending such as Inc. or Ltd. You can also skip the name and use a numbered company.
  • Prepare the company's articles (its rule book) and file an incorporation application. The fee is $350.
  • Every year, file a BC annual report within two months of the anniversary of incorporation. The fee is $43.39. If a company misses it two years in a row, the registrar may dissolve (close) the company.

Federal corporation

  • Incorporating federally with Corporations Canada costs $200 online, and the federal annual return is $12 online.
  • A federal company must also register in each province where it does business. In BC that means registering as an extraprovincial company ($350) and filing BC annual reports as well.

These are government fees only; some online filings add a small service charge. Legal help and a city business licence cost extra. Our guide to BC business licences and registrations covers the other sign-ups.

How each one is taxed

Sole proprietor: on your personal return

You report the business's income and expenses on Form T2125 and include the profit on your personal T1 return. It's added to any other income and taxed at your personal rates. For 2026, the combined federal and BC rate on each extra dollar is 28.2% when taxable income is between $58,523 and $100,728, and it climbs from there. If the business has a loss, it can reduce your other income, such as wages from a part-time job.

Corporation: its own return and its own rates

The company files a T2 corporate return within six months of its year-end, every year, even when it owes no tax. A Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC: a private company controlled by Canadians) can claim the small business deduction on its active business income (profit from actually running the business, not from investments). This lowers the tax rate on the first $500,000 of that income each year, called the "business limit." Rates for 2026:

  • Federal rate with the small business deduction: 9%.
  • BC small business rate: 2%.
  • Combined: about 11% on income up to the business limit, compared with 27% (15% federal plus 12% BC) on income above it.

Companies that are associated (for example, two companies controlled by the same person) must share one $500,000 limit. The limit also shrinks when the group's investment income is between $50,000 and $150,000 a year, and it's gone above $150,000. A company's losses stay inside the company: shareholders can't claim them on their own returns.

The "tax deferral" idea

Here's an illustration with round numbers. Say your business makes $150,000 of profit and you need $90,000 a year to live on.

  • As a sole proprietor, all $150,000 is taxed on your personal return this year. The last $60,000 is taxed at combined 2026 rates that rise from 28.2% to 40.7%.
  • With a corporation, you could pay yourself $90,000 and leave $60,000 in the company. At about 11%, the company pays roughly $6,600 of tax, leaving about $53,400 to reinvest or save.
Deferral isn't the same as saving. When you later take that money out as a dividend, you pay personal tax on it. The benefit comes mainly from timing, so it helps most when you don't need all the profit to live on. If you take out everything each year, incorporating may save little or nothing once the extra costs are counted.

Paying yourself and CPP

A sole proprietor simply takes money out of the business. These "drawings" aren't a deduction: you're taxed on the profit whether you take it out or not. With a corporation, the money belongs to the company, so you need a proper way to pay yourself. The two main options:

Salary (or bonus)

  • The company runs payroll, deducts tax and CPP, and gives you a T4 slip.
  • You pay the employee share of CPP and the company pays a matching employer share, which builds your future CPP pension.
  • Salary counts as earned income, so it creates RRSP room: 18% of last year's earned income, up to the yearly maximum ($33,810 for 2026).

Dividends

  • A dividend is a share of the company's after-tax profit paid to shareholders. It's reported on a T5 slip.
  • Dividends are a return on your shares, not pay for work, so no CPP is deducted. That saves cash now but builds no CPP pension.
  • Dividends aren't on the CRA's list of earned income, so they create no RRSP room.
  • On your return, dividends are "grossed up" (increased) and then reduced by the dividend tax credit. "Eligible" dividends are grossed up by 38% and "other than eligible" dividends by 15%.

CPP for the self-employed

A sole proprietor pays both halves of CPP on net business income. For 2026, that's 11.9% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600 (up to $8,460.90), plus 8% on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000 (up to $832). The CRA has posted the 2027 figures: the first earnings ceiling rises to $76,700 with a self-employed maximum of $8,418, and the second ceiling rises to $87,400 with a maximum of $856.

Many owners use a mix of salary and dividends. Our guide to paying yourself from a corporation goes into more detail.

Liability protection and the extra work of a corporation

How much protection does a company really give?

A shareholder's liability is generally limited to what they put into the company, so a supplier with a claim against the business usually can't take your house. But there are important limits:

  • Personal guarantees. Banks and landlords often ask a small company's owner to personally promise to pay a loan or lease. If you sign, you owe that debt if the company can't pay.
  • Directors' liability. Directors can be personally liable, along with the company, for payroll deductions and GST/HST the company should have withheld, collected or sent to the CRA but didn't.

Extra costs and filings

  • A T2 return every year, due six months after year-end. Tax owing is generally due two months after year-end, or three months for many CCPCs claiming the small business deduction.
  • The BC annual report (plus the federal annual return if federally incorporated).
  • A separate business bank account and proper bookkeeping, since the company's money isn't yours.
  • T4 slips if you pay salary (due by the end of February) and T5 slips if you pay dividends. See our guide to payroll in BC.
  • Company records and higher accounting fees than for a simple T2125.

Selling your business one day

Owners who sell shares of a qualified small business corporation may be able to shelter a large capital gain with the lifetime capital gains exemption. The CRA's guidance shows a limit of $1,250,000 for 2025, notes it came from proposed changes, and says the limit is indexed to inflation. The conditions are strict and need planning years ahead. A sole proprietor has no shares to sell.

Sales taxes apply either way

Sole proprietor or corporation, you must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales (together with associated businesses) go over $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over the last four quarters. Taxi and ride-share drivers must register no matter how much they earn. In BC, accounting, architectural, engineering and geoscience, non-residential real estate and security services became subject to PST on October 1, 2026, so those businesses must register to collect it. See our GST/HST and PST guide.

Changes proposed in the 2026 federal budget

The federal budget released on November 5, 2026 proposed several changes that matter to business owners.

  • One filing date: May 15. Starting with the 2027 tax year, all individuals would file and pay by May 15. Today, self-employed people and their spouses or partners file by June 15 but must pay by April 30. Under the proposal, self-employed people would file a month earlier than now but pay two weeks later.
  • Higher instalment threshold. Instalments are tax payments made during the year. The threshold that triggers them would rise from $3,000 to $7,500 for corporations and for individuals outside Quebec, starting with tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2028.
  • One yearly instalment. From the 2028 tax year, people whose main income is from a business could pay one instalment a year instead of four. See our tax instalments guide.
  • Canada Growth Incentive. A new exemption of up to $15 million in capital gains on certain shares of qualifying companies issued from November 5, 2026 and before 2037. The rules are detailed, so get advice before relying on it.
These are proposals, not law yet. They can change before Parliament passes them. Until then, the current deadlines and instalment rules still apply.

Signs it may be time to incorporate

There's no single income level where incorporating becomes right. Weigh these factors:

  • You earn more than you spend. If profit is well above what you need to live on, and you'd leave the extra in the business, the lower corporate rate can help you grow.
  • Your business carries real risk. Contracts, employees, a lease or customers who could sue may make limited liability worth having.
  • You need to raise money. It's usually easier for a corporation to raise capital, for example by selling shares to an investor.
  • You might sell one day. Selling shares of a qualifying company may let you use the lifetime capital gains exemption.
  • Family members are involved. Paying them or making them shareholders brings special tax rules, so get advice first.

Staying a sole proprietor often makes sense when you're just starting, expect early losses (which can reduce your other income), need all the profit to live on, or want to keep costs and paperwork low.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing personal and business money. Paying personal bills from the company account makes the books messy and can cause tax problems.
  • Treating company money as your own. Money you take from a corporation that isn't salary, a dividend or a repaid expense can become a shareholder loan. If it isn't repaid within one year after the end of the company's tax year (and isn't part of a pattern of borrowing and repaying), it can be added to your personal income.
  • Incorporating only for the "11% rate". If you take out every dollar each year, the deferral mostly disappears while the extra costs remain.
  • Forgetting the annual report. Two missed years in a row can get the company dissolved.
  • Not registering a business name. A sole proprietor using any name other than their own legal name must get it approved and register it.
  • Assuming a company protects everything. Personal guarantees and unpaid payroll or GST/HST remittances can still land on you.
  • Planning around proposed deadlines too early. The May 15 date and $7,500 instalment threshold aren't law yet. Keep using today's dates until they are.
