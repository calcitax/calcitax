WorkSafeBC for small businesses: registering, reporting and paying

If you hire even one worker in B.C., you need WorkSafeBC coverage. Here's who must register, what it costs in 2026 and 2027, when to report your 2026 payroll, and how to stay out of trouble with contractors and penalties.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 24, 2026 · Figures for 2026 and 2027

In short

  • If your business hires and pays workers in B.C., full-time, part-time, casual or contract, you must register with WorkSafeBC.
  • Sole proprietors and partners aren't automatically covered; they can buy optional Personal Coverage.
  • Premiums are a rate per $100 of payroll that depends on your industry; the average base rate is $1.55 for both 2026 and 2027.
  • Each worker's earnings count only up to $127,500 for 2026 and $130,800 for 2027.
  • Small employers report 2026 payroll once, in early March 2027; larger ones report quarterly and reconcile by February 28.
  • Get clearance letters before and after using contractors, and report injuries within three days.

What WorkSafeBC is and why it matters

WorkSafeBC is British Columbia's workers' compensation board. Employers pay premiums (insurance payments) into a shared system. If a worker is hurt or becomes ill because of their job, WorkSafeBC pays benefits such as health care and wage-loss payments.

For a business owner, this means two separate jobs:

  • Insurance: register, report your payroll and pay premiums on time.
  • Health and safety: keep your workplace safe and report injuries and certain incidents.

WorkSafeBC is separate from the CRA. Opening a CRA payroll account and paying CPP and EI is a different job from registering with WorkSafeBC and paying its premiums.

Who must register

The basic rule: if you own a business that hires and pays one or more workers in B.C., you must register for WorkSafeBC coverage. It doesn't matter whether the work is full-time, part-time, casual or on contract. All employers need coverage unless they are exempt.

An "employer" can be a self-employed proprietor, a partnership, a corporation, a society or another legal entity. Once you are registered, all your workers are covered, including:

  • some contractors who are really working as your workers (see "Hiring contractors" below), and
  • your children, if they work for you and your business pays them.

Some situations people often miss:

  • Your spouse in a sole proprietorship. If your spouse is a paid worker in your proprietorship, WorkSafeBC says they are not covered. You can apply for spousal coverage separately.
  • Owners of a corporation. Shareholders, directors and officers who actively work in their company are treated as its workers, and the corporation must be registered to cover them. If a company that should be registered isn't, an injured shareholder's claim can be rejected.
  • Hiring help at home. Homeowners and renters who hire their own worker may need to register, depending on the work and the hours. For example, WorkSafeBC sets the line at 15 or more hours a week for before- or after-school child care, 8 or more hours a week for ongoing help such as a housekeeper, gardener or full-time nanny, and 24 or more hours in total for a one-time project.

Not sure? Read WorkSafeBC's "who needs coverage" pages or call its Employer Service Centre. If you apply and WorkSafeBC decides you're not eligible, it sends you a letter explaining why.

Coverage for you as the owner

Registering your business covers your workers. It may not cover you.

  • Sole proprietors and partners are not covered automatically. You may be able to buy optional Personal Coverage (formerly called Personal Optional Protection). Sole proprietors can buy it with or without workers.
  • Owners or shareholders of an incorporated company who work for it can't buy Personal Coverage. Their protection comes through the company's registration.

With Personal Coverage you choose a monthly coverage amount. When you apply online, you can pick an amount between $3,100 and $5,000 a month; higher amounts need a separate form (Form 1801). Your monthly premium is the coverage amount multiplied by the premium rate for your type of business. WorkSafeBC's own example, a courier who supplies their own vehicle: at a 3.87% rate, $3,100 of monthly coverage costs $119.97 a month. If you're injured at work, wage-loss benefits are generally based on 90% of the coverage amount you chose, less deductions.

Coverage starts the day WorkSafeBC receives your completed application, or on a later date you choose. It stays in place until you or WorkSafeBC cancel it, and it is cancelled if premiums aren't paid on time. WorkSafeBC doesn't mail payment reminders, so managing payments online is the safest way to stay on track.

How to register

Most businesses apply online. You create an online services profile (or log in), and the application saves as you go. WorkSafeBC says it takes about 20 minutes, and you get a confirmation number by email. You can also send the paper Employer's Registration Application (Form 1800), but that may take longer.

Have this ready:

If you are a...You'll typically need
CorporationCRA business number, mailing and work addresses, start date, what you sell with each item's share of revenue, active shareholders' details, and estimated payroll for workers and active shareholders

WorkSafeBC says a review can take about 10 business days, and it recommends applying 30 days before you start your business or hire a worker. If you're eligible, your letter confirms your account number, your classification unit (the industry group you're placed in), the date your coverage starts and your premium rate.

How premiums are calculated, and your 2027 rate

Your premium is a rate per $100 of assessable payroll, which is basically the wages you pay your workers.

  • Your rate depends on your industry. WorkSafeBC places each business in a "classification unit" with similar businesses, and groups those units into "rate groups." For 2026 there are 512 classification units in 55 rate groups; for 2027 there are 511 units in 56 rate groups. Rates differ by industry because each group's rate reflects the cost of injuries in that kind of work.
  • The average base rate is $1.55 per $100 of payroll for both 2026 and 2027. WorkSafeBC says 2027 is the tenth year in a row at this level. Your own rate can be higher or lower.
  • Earnings are capped per worker. WorkSafeBC counts each person's earnings only up to the maximum wage rate (also called maximum assessable earnings): $127,500 for 2026 and $130,800 for 2027. Anything above that isn't assessable.
  • Your safety record matters. Through "experience rating," employers whose claim costs are below their rate group's average can earn discounts of up to 50% over time; higher-than-average costs can mean surcharges of up to 100% over time.

Assessable payroll includes wages and salaries paid to workers, including active shareholders. It can also include payments to contractors who count as your workers. Since January 1, 2026, the only tips you report are verifiable ones: tips that show up in electronic payment records or on a worker's T4. Cash tips no longer need to be reported unless you already put them on a T4.

A simple illustration: if your industry rate were the average $1.55 and your payroll for the year were $100,000, your premium would be about $1,550 ($100,000 ÷ 100 × $1.55). Your real rate will differ, so always check it.

What's changing for 2027

Each industry's rate changes from year to year even when the average stays the same. WorkSafeBC says that for 2027, 30% of B.C. employers will see their industry base rate go down, 48% will see it go up and 22% will see no change. For 2026 and 2027, WorkSafeBC limited base rate increases to 15% and allowed decreases of up to 30% (the usual limit is 20% either way).

WorkSafeBC also announced about $283 million in assessment credits for 2027, going to roughly 31,000 employers in 15 industry groups that have large surpluses.

To find your 2027 rate:

  • Use WorkSafeBC's "Find your classification unit, industry, or rate" search tool, which now includes 2027 rates.
  • Log in to online services and choose "View rate information" on the Insurance tab to see your account's rates and claim costs.

Budget tip: Look up your 2027 rate now and multiply it by your expected 2027 payroll (capped at $130,800 per worker) so the bill isn't a surprise.

Reporting payroll and paying premiums

How often you report depends on the size of your premiums:

  • Annual reporters: employers with premiums under $2,000 a year report and pay once a year.
  • Quarterly reporters: employers with premiums over $2,000 a year, or in classifications where quarterly reporting is mandatory, report and pay every three months, then file a year-end reconciliation (a summary that matches the year's quarterly reports to actual payroll).

So for 2026 payroll, annual reporters will file in early March 2027, and quarterly reporters have their fourth-quarter report due in January 2027 and their reconciliation due in February 2027. Watch your email in January for your exact date.

You report and pay through WorkSafeBC's employer online services. Without an online account, "Fast File & Pay" handles basic reporting and payment for the current year and one year before. You can pay by credit card (up to $10,000 per account every 30 days), through your bank (allow five days for processing) or by cheque (allow mailing time plus five days). Keeping your T4 totals, WorkSafeBC payroll report and bookkeeping in step makes the numbers easy to check.

Hiring contractors: workers, independent operators and clearance letters

Calling someone a "contractor" doesn't decide how WorkSafeBC sees them. A contractor is likely your worker if they don't run an independent business, for example if they supply only their labour, supply labour plus minor materials, or work regular hours for you on an ongoing basis. In that case you pay premiums for them.

A contractor looks more like an independent business if they have contracts with several clients, advertise to the public and bring their own major equipment.

Even when you hire a registered contractor, you could be liable for premiums tied to their work for you if they fall behind on their WorkSafeBC payments. The protection is a clearance letter, which shows whether a firm is registered and up to date:

  • WorkSafeBC advises getting one before and after a contractor works for you.
  • You can search online by the firm's WorkSafeBC account number or its exact legal or trade name, for up to 150 firms at once. Through online services you can also get email alerts when a firm's clearance status changes.
  • Before buying another business's assets, contact WorkSafeBC: if the seller owes premiums, the assets may be subject to a lien (a legal claim) in WorkSafeBC's favour.

A contractor may be registered for their workers but have declined Personal Coverage for themselves. WorkSafeBC warns you could face a lawsuit if that person is hurt on your job. Privacy rules stop WorkSafeBC from telling you whether someone has Personal Coverage, so ask the contractor for proof.

Penalties, injuries and your safety duties

  • Late reports or payments may be charged a penalty based on your premiums. The minimum penalty is $50.
  • Unpaid balances are charged a 1% overdue penalty each month.
  • Under-reporting payroll or under-paying premiums can mean an 8% penalty.
  • Not registering when you should is against the law. If an uninsured worker (including a shareholder) is injured or becomes ill from work, you could have to pay both the claim costs and the premiums you owe.

WorkSafeBC reduces or cancels penalties only for extraordinary reasons, so it's far cheaper to register early and file on time.

When someone gets hurt

  • Report a workplace injury or illness to WorkSafeBC within three days of the incident.
  • Get the worker to medical treatment and pay for that transportation.
  • Report certain incidents immediately, even if nobody was hurt, then investigate and send WorkSafeBC a full investigation report within 30 days.
  • Keep the workplace safe. Employers must follow the Workers Compensation Act and the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation.
  • Tell WorkSafeBC about changes, such as closing the business, a change in the legal entity that runs it (for example, incorporating a sole proprietorship), or new phone, email or mailing details.

Checklist and common mistakes

Before your first hire

  • Apply to WorkSafeBC about 30 days before your first worker starts. Our guide to hiring your first employee in B.C. covers the CRA side.
  • Decide whether you want Personal Coverage for yourself (sole proprietors and partners).
  • Note your premium rate and whether you're an annual or quarterly reporter.

This winter

  • Look up your 2027 rate and budget for it.
  • Watch for WorkSafeBC's January email with your annual report due date, and file 2026 payroll on time.
  • Cap each worker at $127,500 for 2026 and $130,800 for 2027.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a CRA payroll account also registers you with WorkSafeBC.
  • Forgetting working shareholders or family members in payroll.
  • Treating workers as contractors to avoid premiums.
  • Using a contractor without checking clearance before and after.
  • Still reporting cash tips that aren't on a T4, or missing tips that are.
  • Reporting injuries late, or not at all.
