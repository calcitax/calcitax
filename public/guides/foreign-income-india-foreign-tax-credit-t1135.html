Income from India or abroad while living in Canada

Still have a flat, fixed deposits or investments back in India? Once you live in Canada, the income they earn belongs on your Canadian tax return. This guide explains what to report, how to convert rupees, how to get credit for tax paid in India, and when you must tell the CRA what you own abroad.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 8, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Canadian residents report income from everywhere, including rent, interest, dividends and gains from India, even if the money stays there.
  • Convert amounts to Canadian dollars using Bank of Canada rates, and rebuild India's April-to-March figures into a January-to-December year.
  • A foreign tax credit on Form T2209 usually stops you paying tax twice, but it is capped at the Canadian tax on that income.
  • File Form T1135 if the cost of your foreign property was more than $100,000 at any time in the year, except in your first year as a resident.
  • Late T1135 filing costs $25 a day, up to $2,500 a year, and bigger penalties can apply.
  • Missed past years? The CRA's Voluntary Disclosures Program may cancel penalties if you come forward first.

Canada taxes residents on income from everywhere

If you live in Canada for tax purposes, you are a Canadian tax resident. Once you are a resident, the CRA (Canada Revenue Agency) expects you to report your world income. That means income from every source, inside and outside Canada, on your Canadian tax return.

This surprises many families who still have money or property in India. Income does not become "Canadian" only when you bring it here. Rent paid into an Indian bank account, interest that stays in a fixed deposit, and a gain on a flat sold in Pune all count, even if the money never leaves India.

Two other points to know from the start:

  • India may tax the same income. Canada and India have a tax treaty (an agreement between the two countries) that decides which country can tax which kind of income, and how much. Canada then gives you a credit for Indian tax so you are not taxed twice on the same income.
  • You may also have to report what you own abroad, not just what it earns, on a form called T1135. We cover it below.

If you arrived recently, our first-year guide for newcomers explains when your residency starts. If you are moving back to India, see our guide to leaving Canada.

Common kinds of income from India

Here is how the most common types of Indian income are treated. The treaty column shows what the Canada–India tax treaty says about India's right to tax it.

IncomeOn your Canadian returnCan India tax it too?
Rent from a flat or house in IndiaReport the rent and claim allowable expenses on Form T776, the rental statementYes. Income from real estate can be taxed where the property is
Interest on fixed deposits (FDs), savings, NRO or NRE accountsReport on line 12100 every yearYes, but the treaty caps India's tax at 15% of the gross interest
Dividends from Indian shares or mutual fundsReport on line 12100. Foreign dividends don't get the Canadian dividend tax creditYes, but for individuals the treaty caps India's tax at 25% of the gross dividend
Gain on selling property, shares or mutual fund unitsReport as a capital gain. Under current law, half of the gain is taxedYes. The treaty lets both countries tax most gains
Pension paid from IndiaReport it, then deduct the treaty-exempt part on line 25600The treaty says a pension arising in India is taxable only in India

A few things that catch people out

  • Interest on an FD is reported as it is earned, not only when the deposit matures. For a deposit that compounds over several years, the CRA wants you to report the interest earned in each full investment year, even though you haven't received it.
  • Interest from all your Indian accounts counts, whether or not India taxed it.
  • Rental property in India follows the same basic rules as a Canadian rental. See our rental income guide for which expenses you can claim.
  • Selling property in India? Our capital gains guide explains how the gain is worked out. The federal government cancelled the proposed increase to the share of gains that is taxed, so it stays at one-half.
  • Pensions need care. Whether a payment counts as a "pension" under the treaty is not always obvious, especially for lump-sum withdrawals. Ask for help before you leave one off your return.

Converting rupees and matching India's April-to-March year

Use Canadian dollars

Everything on your return must be in Canadian dollars. The Income Tax Act says a foreign amount is generally converted using the Bank of Canada exchange rate for the day the amount arose. In practice:

  • One-time amounts, such as the sale price of a flat, use the Bank of Canada rate on the day of that transaction. The cost of the flat uses the rate on the day you bought it.
  • Amounts paid throughout the year, such as a monthly pension, can use the Bank of Canada annual average rate. The CRA says an average may also be acceptable for other income in some situations.
  • Indian tax you paid should be converted at the same rate you used for the income it relates to.

The Bank of Canada publishes an annual average for the Indian rupee. For 2025 it was 0.01605 Canadian dollars per rupee.

Illustration only: if you received rent of ₹6,00,000 (600,000 rupees) spread over 2025, you could convert it as 600,000 × 0.01605 = $9,630.

India's year is different from Canada's

India's tax year runs from April 1 to March 31. Canada's tax year for individuals is the calendar year, January 1 to December 31. Indian bank certificates and TDS statements (TDS means tax deducted at source, the tax a bank or tenant withholds before paying you) usually follow the Indian year.

For your Canadian return, you need to rebuild your figures for January to December. That means taking the last nine months of one Indian year and the first three months of the next. The CRA also expects Indian tax to be split across calendar years the same way, so the credit lines up with the income.

Tip: Ask your Indian bank for monthly or quarterly interest statements, not just the yearly certificate. They make the calendar-year split much easier.

Getting credit for tax paid in India

When India taxes income that you also report in Canada, you can usually claim a foreign tax credit. A tax credit reduces the tax you owe, dollar for dollar. You work out the federal credit on Form T2209, Federal Foreign Tax Credits, and enter it on line 40500. B.C. residents work out the provincial credit on Form T2036.

The basic limit

For each country, the credit is generally the lower of:

  • the foreign tax you actually paid on that income, and
  • the Canadian tax you would otherwise pay on that same income.

So the credit can cancel the Canadian tax on your Indian income, but it can never reduce the tax on your Canadian income.

Illustration only: your Indian FDs earned interest worth $10,000 and the bank withheld $1,000 of TDS. If Canadian tax on that $10,000 would be $2,500, your credit is $1,000 and you pay the remaining $1,500 in Canada. If Canadian tax would only be $800, your credit is limited to $800.

Other rules to know

  • 15% cap on investment income. For interest and dividends (but not rent or gains from real estate), an individual's credit is limited to 15% of the gross income. Foreign tax above that may be deducted from income instead.
  • Only tax you really owed counts. If India withheld more than the treaty allows, the extra is not a foreign tax for Canadian purposes. You would need to ask India for a refund of it.
  • Refunds reduce the credit. If you get Indian tax back, for example after filing an Indian return, only the tax you finally paid counts.
  • No carry-forward. Unused credit on this kind of income (called non-business income) can't be saved for another year.
  • Keep proof. Keep TDS certificates, Indian returns and payment receipts. If you file on paper, the CRA asks you to attach receipts and your calculations. Documents in a language other than English or French need an acceptable translation.
Watch out: Don't subtract Indian tax from the income you report. Report the full amount before tax, then claim the credit separately.

Form T1135: reporting what you own outside Canada

Form T1135, the Foreign Income Verification Statement, is an information form. It doesn't create tax by itself. It tells the CRA what foreign investments you hold.

Who must file

You must file if you are a Canadian resident and the total cost of your specified foreign property was more than $100,000 at any time in the year. "At any time" matters: if you crossed $100,000 for one week, you file, even if you cashed out before December 31.

"Cost" generally means what you paid, not today's value. A flat bought for a large price with a small down payment still counts at its full cost. For property you owned when you first became a resident, the cost is its fair market value on the day you became a resident. For inherited or gifted property, it is the value when you received it.

Good to know: You don't file T1135 for the year you first became a resident of Canada. The rules apply from your second tax year here.

What counts

  • Money held outside Canada, such as Indian savings accounts, NRO and NRE accounts and fixed deposits
  • Shares of foreign companies, even if you hold them through a Canadian broker
  • Indian mutual funds (a fund based outside Canada counts, even though its own holdings are not listed)
  • Real estate outside Canada that you rent out or hold as an investment
  • Money owed to you by people or companies outside Canada, such as foreign bonds or loans

What doesn't count

  • Personal-use property, such as a home in India used mainly (more than half the time) by you for personal use. A flat that is mostly rented out for profit does count.
  • Property used only in an active business you run
  • Investments inside your RRSP, TFSA or other registered plans
  • Canadian mutual funds, even if they invest in foreign shares

Simplified or detailed

  • Part A (simplified): if your total cost was more than $100,000 but under $250,000 throughout the year, you tick the type of property you hold, the countries, and report your total income and gains.
  • Part B (detailed): if your total cost reached $250,000 or more at any time, you list each property.

When it's due

T1135 is due on the same day as your tax return. For 2026, that is April 30, 2027, or June 15, 2027 if you or your spouse or common-law partner ran a business. You can file it electronically along with your return.

Penalties, and what to do if you missed past years

T1135 penalties

  • Filing late: $25 a day for up to 100 days, with a minimum of $100 and a maximum of $2,500 per year.
  • Knowingly or through gross negligence (serious carelessness) not filing: $500 a month for up to 24 months (up to $12,000), rising to $1,000 a month (up to $24,000) if you ignore a CRA demand to file.
  • After 24 months: an extra penalty of 5% of the cost of the foreign property can apply, minus penalties already charged.

There's another cost. If you didn't report income from foreign property and didn't file T1135 on time, the CRA gets three extra years to reassess (review and change) that year's return.

If you missed income or T1135 in past years

Many people only learn about these rules years after arriving. The fix is to correct the past years, ideally before the CRA contacts you.

The CRA's Voluntary Disclosures Program (VDP) lets you correct errors or omissions. If you qualify, the CRA may cancel penalties and part of the interest. You still pay the tax owed. The program was changed on October 1, 2025. To qualify, you must meet all five conditions the CRA lists today:

  1. You apply before an audit or investigation has started about the information you are disclosing.
  2. You include all the information and documents for the years involved.
  3. The error or omission carries interest or penalties.
  4. The information is at least one year past its due date.
  5. You pay the estimated tax owing, or ask for a payment arrangement.

The CRA gives more relief to people who come forward on their own than to those who act after being contacted. Information that is less than a year overdue doesn't qualify for the VDP, so it is usually fixed by changing the return instead. Talk to a professional about which route fits.

Checklist

  1. List every Indian account, deposit, property, share and mutual fund you own, with what you paid for each.
  2. Collect interest certificates, rent records, sale papers and TDS certificates for both Indian years that overlap the Canadian year.
  3. Rebuild your income for January to December, including FD interest that has built up but not been paid.
  4. Convert each amount to Canadian dollars with Bank of Canada rates, and use the same rate for the related Indian tax.
  5. Report the full amount before Indian tax, then claim the credit on T2209 and T2036.
  6. Check whether India withheld more than the treaty allows, and ask India for a refund of any extra.
  7. Add up the cost of your specified foreign property. If it went over $100,000 at any time, file T1135 with your return.
  8. File by April 30, 2027 for the 2026 year (June 15, 2027 if you or your spouse ran a business).
  9. Keep all records, with translations where needed.
  10. Missed earlier years? Get advice on fixing them before the CRA contacts you.
