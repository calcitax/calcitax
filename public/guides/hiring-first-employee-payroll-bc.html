Hiring your first employee in BC: payroll made simple

Hiring your first employee is a big step, and it comes with new duties: taking CPP, EI and income tax off each pay and sending it to the CRA, following BC's employment rules, and filing slips at year-end. Here's what to do, in order, with the new rates for pay starting January 1, 2027.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 18, 2026 · Figures for 2026 and 2027

In short

  • Check the worker is truly an employee; the CRA looks at control, tools, financial risk and chance of profit, not just the contract.
  • Open a CRA payroll (RP) account before your first remittance is due, and register with WorkSafeBC; it suggests applying about four weeks before you hire.
  • For 2027 pay, CPP is 5.75% each for you and the employee (down from 5.95%), and EI is $1.64 per $100 for the employee, with you paying 1.4 times that.
  • Most new employers remit by the 15th of the following month; late remittances over $500 carry penalties of 3% to 10%.
  • BC requires at least $18.25 an hour (since June 1, 2026), pay at least twice a month, and 4% vacation pay after 5 days.
  • File 2026 T4 slips by March 1, 2027, and issue a Record of Employment when someone leaves or stops being paid.

Employee or contractor? Get this right first

Before you set up payroll, make sure the person really is an employee. Calling someone a contractor does not make them one. Outside Quebec, the CRA looks at the working relationship in two steps.

  1. What did you both intend? A written contract helps show this, but it is not the final word.
  2. Do the facts match? The CRA looks at who controls the work and the hours, who supplies and pays for tools and equipment, whether the worker can hire helpers, whether the worker takes on financial risk and has money invested in their own business, and whether they can make a profit or a loss.

Example: A shop assistant who works the hours you set, uses your till and is paid by the hour is almost certainly an employee. A bookkeeper who serves several clients, sets their own schedule, uses their own software and sends you invoices is more likely self-employed.

If you get it wrong, you can be charged for the CPP, EI and tax you should have deducted, plus penalties. If you are unsure, you or the worker can ask the CRA for a ruling, online through your CRA account or by mailing Form CPT1. The request must reach the CRA by June 29 of the year after the year the work was done.

Set up your accounts before the first payday

1. A CRA payroll account

You need a payroll program account with the CRA. It is attached to your business number (BN, the nine-digit number the CRA gives your business) and ends in "RP". If you already have a BN, for example for GST, add the payroll account to it rather than getting a new one. You can register online through the CRA's Business Registration Online service, or by mailing Form RC1 if you can't register online.

You must open the payroll account before your first remittance is due. For most new employers, that is the 15th of the month after the month you first pay someone.

2. WorkSafeBC

In BC, if your business hires one or more workers, whether full-time, part-time, casual or on contract, you must register with WorkSafeBC for workers' compensation insurance. WorkSafeBC suggests applying about four weeks before you hire. If you should be registered and aren't, you could be billed for the full cost of a worker's injury claim.

Your premium is your rate times your assessable payroll. Rates depend on your industry and claims history; the 2027 average base rate is unchanged at $1.55 per $100 of payroll. A sole proprietor's spouse is not covered automatically. See our WorkSafeBC guide for details.

3. BC Employer Health Tax (usually not yet)

BC's Employer Health Tax only applies once your total BC payroll for the calendar year is over $1,000,000 (or $1,500,000 for a registered charity or non-profit). A business hiring its first employee will almost always be below that, so you don't need to register. If you ever go over, you must register by December 31 of the first year you owe the tax.

Paperwork for a new employee

  • Social Insurance Number (SIN): get the employee's SIN within 3 days of when they start work. You can be charged a $100 penalty if you can't show you made a reasonable effort to get it.
  • SINs starting with 9: these are given to people who are not citizens or permanent residents, such as newcomers on work or study permits. They are only valid until the expiry date on the person's immigration document, so check that date and ask for an updated document when it runs out.
  • Form TD1 (federal): every new employee fills one out. It tells you which tax credits they are claiming, which affects how much tax you deduct.
  • Form TD1BC (provincial): needed if a BC employee claims more than the basic personal amount.

Keep the TD1 forms with your payroll records; do not send them to the CRA. If an employee doesn't give you a TD1, deduct tax as if they claim only the basic personal amount. Employees need a new form when their credits change, and should give it to you within 7 days of the change.

Also put the pay rate, hours and start date in writing.

Payroll deductions: CPP, EI and income tax

Each payday you take three main deductions off the employee's gross pay (their pay before deductions), and you add your own employer share on top.

Canada Pension Plan (CPP)

For 2027, the employee pays 5.75% of pensionable earnings above a $3,500 yearly basic exemption, up to the year's maximum pensionable earnings (YMPE) of $76,700. The most an employee pays is $4,209, and you pay the same amount again as the employer. The rate is lower than in 2026 because a law passed in June 2026 cut the base part of CPP from 4.95% to 4.75% each, starting in 2027.

CPP2 (the second additional contribution) applies only to earnings between $76,700 and $87,400 in 2027, at 4% each for employee and employer. The most each side pays is $428. It only matters for employees earning more than $76,700.

Employment Insurance (EI)

For 2027, the employee rate is $1.64 per $100 of insurable earnings, up to maximum insurable earnings of $70,800. That works out to a maximum of $1,161.12 for the employee. As the employer, you pay 1.4 times the employee's premium, up to $1,625.57.

Income tax

You also deduct federal and BC income tax, based on the employee's pay and their TD1 forms. The easiest way is the CRA's free Payroll Deductions Online Calculator (PDOC), which works out CPP, EI and federal and provincial tax for each pay. Payroll software does the same.

Example (an illustration with round numbers): You hire someone at $50,000 a year in 2027. Their CPP is about $2,674 (5.75% of $50,000 minus $3,500) and their EI is $820. You pay the same $2,674 of CPP plus $1,148 of EI (1.4 times $820). That is about $3,822 a year on top of the salary, before WorkSafeBC and vacation pay.

Watch January 1: the CPP rate goes down and the EI rate and ceilings go up for 2027. Make sure your payroll software has its 2027 update, or use the January 2027 version of PDOC, before your first payroll of the new year.

Sending deductions to the CRA (remitting)

The money you deduct belongs to the government. You hold it in trust and must send it, with your employer share, by the due date.

  • Regular remitters: the CRA must receive the payment by the 15th of the month after you paid the employee. Wages paid in March are due April 15. A new employer who doesn't qualify for quarterly remitting is a regular remitter.
  • New small employers: if your payroll account has been open less than 12 months, your total deductions for a month are under $1,000, and you have a perfect compliance record on all your payroll and GST/HST accounts for the past 12 months, you can remit quarterly instead: by April 15, July 15, October 15 and January 15.
  • If a due date falls on a weekend or public holiday, the payment is on time if it arrives by the next business day.

Penalties

Late or missing remittances over $500 get a penalty based on how late they are:

How latePenalty
1 to 3 days3%
4 or 5 days5%
6 or 7 days7%
More than 7 days, or not remitted10%

A second or later failure in the same year that was made knowingly or through gross negligence can cost 20%. Separately, if you don't deduct CPP, EI or tax at all, the CRA can charge 10% of what you should have deducted. Interest, compounded daily, is added to unpaid amounts and penalties.

Tip: remit when you run payroll rather than waiting for the deadline. A single late payment breaks the perfect compliance record you need for quarterly remitting.

BC employment standards basics

BC's Employment Standards Act sets minimum rules for most employees. The key ones for a new employer:

  • Minimum wage: the general minimum wage is $18.25 an hour since June 1, 2026. Each year it rises by the same percentage as BC inflation (the BC Consumer Price Index), and it stays the same if prices fall.
  • Paydays: pay employees at least twice a month, and within 8 days after the end of each pay period. A pay period can be no longer than 16 days.
  • Vacation pay: employees who have worked for you more than 5 calendar days earn vacation pay of at least 4% of their wages, rising to 6% after 5 years in a row. They also get 2 weeks of vacation after 12 months, and 3 weeks after 5 years. Vacation pay is due at least 7 days before the vacation starts, or on each pay cheque if you both agree in writing.
  • Statutory holidays: BC has 11: New Year's Day, Family Day, Good Friday, Victoria Day, Canada Day, B.C. Day, Labour Day, National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, Thanksgiving, Remembrance Day and Christmas Day. An employee qualifies for stat holiday pay (an average day's pay) after being employed for 30 calendar days and working or earning wages on 15 of the 30 days before the holiday.

Other rules cover overtime, breaks, leaves and ending employment; the BC Employment Standards Branch can help.

Taxable benefits in brief

Pay isn't only wages. If you give an employee something of personal value, it may be a taxable benefit that you must add to their income on payroll and on their T4.

  • Common examples: personal use of a company car, free or cheap housing, and allowances or reimbursements for personal expenses.
  • Cash benefits (paid by cheque or direct deposit) are generally subject to CPP, EI and income tax.
  • Non-cash benefits (things given rather than money) are generally subject to CPP and income tax, but not EI.

The CRA's Guide T4130 covers each type of benefit.

Year-end slips and when someone leaves

T4 slips

After each calendar year, prepare a T4 slip for each employee showing their pay and deductions, and a T4 Summary of the totals. File them with the CRA and give each employee their copy. The deadline is the last day of February. For 2026 pay, that would be February 28, 2027, but it is a Sunday, so the slips are on time if filed by Monday, March 1, 2027. If you have more than 5 slips, you must file electronically.

Record of Employment (ROE)

When an employee stops working for you or has an interruption of earnings (generally 7 days in a row with no work and no pay, for example after a layoff, quitting or a long sick leave), you must issue a Record of Employment to Service Canada. Issue one even if the employee doesn't plan to apply for EI.

  • Electronic ROE (through ROE Web or payroll software): for weekly, every-two-weeks or twice-a-month pay, no later than 5 calendar days after the end of the pay period in which the interruption happened. Monthly pay has a slightly different rule.
  • Paper ROE: within 5 calendar days of the interruption, or of when you learn about it.

Records, common mistakes and a checklist

Records to keep

Keep payroll records (hours, pay, deductions, TD1 forms, remittances, T4s and ROEs) for at least 6 years after the year they relate to. That also covers BC's 4-year rule for employee payroll records.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Treating a worker as a contractor when the facts point to employment.
  • Forgetting the employer share of CPP and EI when budgeting for a new hire.
  • Paying remittances late, even by a day or two.
  • Using last year's rates after January 1.
  • Paying only once a month, which breaks BC's twice-a-month rule.
  • Forgetting vacation pay or stat holiday pay.
  • Not registering with WorkSafeBC before the first hire.

Checklist for your first hire

  1. Confirm the worker is an employee, not a contractor.
  2. Open a CRA payroll (RP) account and register with WorkSafeBC.
  3. Collect the SIN, TD1 and (if needed) TD1BC.
  4. Choose payroll software or use PDOC, and set at least twice-monthly paydays.
  5. Pay at least BC minimum wage, plus vacation and stat holiday pay.
  6. Remit deductions plus your share by the 15th of the next month (or quarterly, if eligible).
  7. Switch to the 2027 CPP and EI rates for pay dated January 1, 2027 or later.
  8. File T4s by the deadline (March 1, 2027 for 2026 pay) and issue an ROE when employment ends.

If you'd rather not handle payroll yourself, a tax professional can help set it up correctly from the first pay.
