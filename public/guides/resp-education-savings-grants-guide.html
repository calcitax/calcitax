RESPs and education grants for your children

A registered education savings plan (RESP) is one of the few places where the government adds free money to your savings. For B.C. families, that can mean a 20% federal grant on what you put in, extra help for lower-income families, and a one-time $1,200 provincial grant. Here's how it all works in plain language, with 2026 figures.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 5, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • The Canada Education Savings Grant adds 20% to your RESP contributions, up to $500 a year and $7,200 per child over a lifetime.
  • Families with lower or middle incomes get an extra 10% or 20% on the first $500 they contribute each year (2026 income bands).
  • The Canada Learning Bond pays up to $2,000 to children from low-income families, with no contribution needed.
  • B.C. children can get a one-time $1,200 grant, but only if you apply between their 6th birthday and the day before they turn 9.
  • The lifetime contribution limit is $50,000 per child, and going over costs 1% a month in tax.
  • Your contributions come back tax-free; grants and growth are taxed to the student, who often pays little or no tax.

What an RESP is

A registered education savings plan (RESP) is a special savings account for a child's education after high school. That can include university, college and other designated schools in Canada, and many schools outside Canada. The money inside grows without being taxed each year. Government grants are paid straight into it.

A few words you will see often:

  • Subscriber: the person who opens the RESP and puts money in. Any adult can do this, including parents, grandparents, other relatives and friends.
  • Beneficiary: the child (or adult) the savings are for.
  • Promoter: the company that offers the RESP, such as a bank, credit union, financial planner or group plan dealer.

Types of plans

  • Family plan: can have more than one beneficiary, which is handy if you have several children. Each beneficiary must be related to the subscriber by blood or adoption, and must be under 21 when added.
  • Individual plan: has one beneficiary. The subscriber doesn't have to be related to the child.
  • Group plan: sold by group plan dealers (sometimes called scholarship plan dealers). The government warns that these plans may charge fees if you stop making regular payments, so read the terms carefully.

Whichever type you choose, ask the promoter about fees for opening, managing or closing the plan, any penalties, and which government grants they offer. Not every promoter offers every grant.

How to open an RESP

  1. Get a Social Insurance Number (SIN) for your child. A SIN is the nine-digit number the government uses to identify you. The child needs one before they can be named on an RESP. The subscriber needs one too.
  2. Choose a promoter. Most banks and credit unions offer RESPs. Compare fees and ask which grants they offer.
  3. Open the plan and name your child. Many promoters let you do this online, by phone or by mail. You don't need a bank account to open an RESP.
  4. Apply for the grants at the same time. Your promoter helps you fill in the application for the Canada Education Savings Grant and the Canada Learning Bond, plus the B.C. grant if your child is the right age.
  5. Contribute if you want the main grant. The Canada Education Savings Grant only matches money you put in. The Canada Learning Bond needs no contribution at all.

Grants are deposited automatically once approved. The federal grant usually arrives about 6 to 8 weeks after your contribution is processed.

The Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG)

The Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) is the federal government's main RESP grant. Every family can get it, whatever their income.

The basic grant

  • The government adds 20% of the first $2,500 you contribute each year, so up to $500 a year.
  • The most one child can receive over their lifetime is $7,200.

The additional grant for lower and middle incomes

On the first $500 you contribute each year, some families get an extra amount. It depends on adjusted family net income, which is roughly the total net income of the child's main caregiver and their spouse or common-law partner.

Adjusted family net income (2026)Extra grant on first $500Most CESG per year
Less than $58,52320% ($100)$600
$58,523 to $117,04510% ($50)$550
More than $117,045None$500

Catching up on missed years

Each child who lives in Canada builds up grant room every year from birth to 17: $500 a year since 2007. If you don't use it, it carries forward. In a year when you have unused room, you can get up to $1,000 of basic grant, which means contributing up to $5,000 that year.

Illustration only: a family that starts saving when their child is 5 puts in $5,000 in one year. The government adds $1,000 (20% of $5,000), using up that year's room plus one missed year.

Rules for 16- and 17-year-olds

Contributions can earn the grant up to the end of the calendar year the child turns 17. But a child who is 16 or 17 only qualifies if, before the end of the year they turned 15, at least one of these happened:

  • at least $2,000 in total was contributed and not taken out, or
  • at least $100 was contributed (and not taken out) in any four years.
Year-end tip: grant room works by calendar year. If you want the 2026 grant, make your contribution by December 31, 2026. Check your promoter's cut-off date, since processing can take a few days.

The Canada Learning Bond (CLB)

The Canada Learning Bond (CLB) is free money for children from low-income families. You don't have to contribute anything to get it. You only need an RESP to receive it.

  • $500 for the first year the child qualifies, plus $100 for each later year they qualify, up to and including the year they turn 15.
  • The most a child can get is $2,000.
  • The child must be born on or after January 1, 2004.
  • The government also pays $25 into the RESP that gets the first $500, to help with the cost of opening it.

Who qualifies

It depends on the adjusted family income of the child's main caregiver and the number of children. For July 1, 2026, to June 30, 2027, the limits are:

  • 1 to 3 children: $58,523 or less
  • 4 children: less than $66,036
  • 5 children: less than $73,577

This is one more reason both parents should file a tax return every year, even with little or no income. Our Canada child benefit guide explains why filing matters for family benefits.

Missed years are paid too

The CLB is retroactive, meaning it pays for past years the child qualified, not just the current one. The main caregiver can ask for it until the day before the child turns 18. After that, the young person can apply themselves from age 18, but must do so before turning 21.

Coming: automatic RESPs

The federal government says that starting in April 2028 it will automatically open an RESP for eligible children born in 2024 or later who don't already have one by age 4, so they get the CLB. Families will be able to opt out starting in 2027. The government says more details are still to come, so treat this as planned rather than final.

B.C. Training and Education Savings Grant (BCTESG)

The B.C. Training and Education Savings Grant (BCTESG) is a one-time $1,200 grant from the Province of British Columbia, paid into your child's RESP.

  • No contribution needed. The province does not ask you to match it.
  • Age window: you can apply from the child's 6th birthday until the day before they turn 9.
  • Residency: both the child and the parent or guardian must live in B.C. when you apply, and the child needs a valid SIN.
  • How to apply: through a financial institution that offers the grant. If your current RESP provider doesn't, you can open a new RESP with one that does.
Don't miss the window: once your child turns 9, the chance to apply for the $1,200 is gone for good. Put a reminder in your calendar for your child's 6th birthday.

Contribution limits and overcontributions

  • There is no yearly limit on how much you can put into an RESP.
  • There is a lifetime limit of $50,000 per child, counting every RESP for that child, no matter who opened it.
  • Only contributions count toward the $50,000. Grants and investment growth don't.

If total contributions go over $50,000, that's an overcontribution. Each subscriber pays a tax of 1% per month on their share of the extra amount until it is taken out.

Overcontributions often happen by accident, for example when grandparents open a separate RESP for the same child. Talk to family members so everyone knows what has already been saved.

How the money comes out

When your child enrols in a qualifying program, the money comes out in two parts that are taxed differently:

Part of the RESPWho gets itTaxed?
Your contributionsYou (or you can pay it to the student)No
Grants, bonds and growth (paid as an EAP)The studentYes, as the student's income

An educational assistance payment (EAP) is a payment from the grants and investment growth. It counts as the student's income, but students often have low income, so they may pay little or no tax on it.

What counts as a qualifying program

  • Full-time in Canada: at least 3 weeks in a row, with at least 10 hours a week of classes or work.
  • Part-time in Canada: at least 3 weeks in a row, with at least 12 hours a month of classes.
  • Full-time outside Canada: at least 13 weeks, or 3 weeks for a university program.

Limits on EAPs

  • Full-time: up to $8,000 in the first 13 weeks in a row of study. After that, there's no set limit while the student keeps qualifying.
  • Part-time: up to $4,000 for each 13-week period.

The student must show official proof of enrolment. EAPs can still be paid for up to 6 months after studies end, if the plan allows it.

If your child doesn't go to school

You have several options, and you don't have to decide right away:

  • Wait. The plan can stay open for many years, in case your child goes back to school later.
  • Change the beneficiary to another child, such as a brother or sister. The CESG can be shared with a sibling who still has grant room.
  • Close the plan. Your contributions come back to you tax-free. The CESG and CLB go back to the government. The CLB can only be used for education.

What happens to the investment growth

The growth can be paid to the subscriber as an accumulated income payment (AIP). This is generally allowed after the year of the plan's 9th anniversary, once every beneficiary is at least 21 and not eligible for EAPs. An AIP is taxed at your regular rate plus an extra 20%.

You can avoid much of that tax by moving up to $50,000 of the growth (over your lifetime) into your RRSP or your spouse's RRSP, if you have enough RRSP contribution room. If your child qualifies for the disability tax credit, the growth may be able to move to their registered disability savings plan (RDSP) instead.

Checklist and common mistakes

Checklist

  • Get a SIN for your child as early as possible.
  • Open an RESP and apply for the CESG and CLB at the same time.
  • Contribute by December 31 each year to use that year's grant room.
  • Apply for the $1,200 BCTESG between your child's 6th and 9th birthdays.
  • File tax returns every year so the government can check your income for the extra grant and the CLB.
  • Keep track of every RESP for each child so no one goes over $50,000.

Common mistakes

  • Not opening an RESP because you can't afford to contribute. The CLB and BCTESG need no contributions.
  • Missing the B.C. grant because the child turned 9.
  • Starting to save only at 16 and finding the child doesn't qualify for the CESG.
  • Signing a group plan without understanding the fees for stopping payments.
  • Letting relatives open a second RESP for the same child without checking the $50,000 total.
