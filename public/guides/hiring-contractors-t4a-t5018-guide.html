Hiring contractors in BC: T4A, T5018 and contractor vs employee

Paying freelancers, trades or other independent contractors is simpler than running payroll, but it still comes with duties. You need to be sure the person really is a contractor, report what you paid them on the right slip, and protect your business from someone else's unpaid WorkSafeBC premiums. Here's how it works for 2026.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 12, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Calling someone a contractor doesn't make them one; the CRA looks at control, tools, risk and chance of profit.
  • Report service fees over $500 a year to a contractor in box 048 of a T4A slip, without GST/HST.
  • Construction businesses report subcontractor payments on a T5018 instead, due six months after their reporting period ends.
  • For 2026 payments, T4A slips are due February 28, 2027; since that is a Sunday, March 1, 2027 is on time.
  • If you file more than 5 slips for a calendar year, you must file them electronically.
  • Get a WorkSafeBC clearance letter before and after a contractor works for you.

First, is the person really a contractor?

An independent contractor (also called self-employed or a subcontractor) runs their own business and sells you a service. An employee works for you under your direction. The label you put on the contract does not settle it. The CRA looks at the real working relationship in two steps:

  1. What did you both intend? A written agreement helps show this.
  2. Do the facts back it up? The CRA checks each of these factors on its own and then together:
  • Control: who decides how, when and where the work is done.
  • Tools and equipment: who supplies and pays for them.
  • Helpers: whether the worker can hire assistants or send someone else to do the job.
  • Financial risk: whether the worker pays their own costs and could lose money.
  • Investment and management: whether the worker has put money into a business of their own.
  • Chance of profit: whether the worker can earn more by pricing jobs well, taking other clients and keeping costs down.

Illustration: a graphic designer who has several clients, sets their own hours, uses their own computer and software, and sends you invoices is likely a contractor. A helper who works the shifts you set, with your tools, at an hourly rate, looks more like an employee.

Getting this wrong is costly. If the CRA later decides the person was an employee, you can owe both the employee and employer shares of CPP and EI that you should have deducted, plus penalties and interest.

If you are unsure, you or the worker can ask the CRA for a CPP/EI ruling (an official decision on the person's status). Payers can ask through My Business Account, or anyone can mail Form CPT1. The deadline is June 29 of the year after the work was done. If the person turns out to be an employee, our guide to hiring your first employee explains payroll.

Before the work starts: collect the right details

A few minutes of paperwork at the start saves a scramble at year-end. For each contractor, keep:

  • Their legal name and address, and whether they are a person, a partnership or a corporation.
  • Their identifying number: a Social Insurance Number (SIN) for an individual, or a business number (BN) for a partnership or corporation. You need this for the year-end slip.
  • Their GST/HST number, if they charge you GST.
  • A written agreement that describes the work, the price and who supplies what.
  • A WorkSafeBC clearance letter (explained below).

Most Canadian-resident contractors are paid in full: you do not take CPP, EI or income tax off their invoices. Paying a contractor who lives outside Canada for work done in Canada follows different rules, including tax withheld at source, so get advice before you pay.

Tip: ask for the SIN or BN when you first sign up a contractor, not in February. The CRA can charge a $100 penalty each time you don't make a real effort to get a SIN. If you truly can't get it, you still file the slip on time without it.

T4A slips: reporting fees for services

A T4A slip is a year-end form that reports certain payments other than salary. When your business pays another business or a self-employed person for services, the yearly total goes in box 048, "Fees for services".

When a slip is needed

  • Under the CRA's administrative policy, you issue a slip when your total payments to that contractor in the calendar year are more than $500, or if you deducted any tax from a payment.
  • Do not include GST/HST or PST in the box 048 amount.
  • Enter the recipient's SIN for an individual, or their business number for a partnership or corporation.
  • You also file a T4A Summary, which totals all your slips. If you don't already have one, you need a CRA payroll (RP) program account to file.

Illustration: you paid a freelance web designer $2,000 plus $100 GST in 2026. You would report $2,000 in box 048 of their T4A, not $2,100.

Due date for 2026 payments

Slips go to the CRA and to each contractor by the last day of February after the year. For 2026 payments, that is February 28, 2027. Because that day is a Sunday, the CRA treats slips received (or postmarked) by Monday, March 1, 2027 as on time.

Good to know: the CRA says it is currently not charging penalties for problems with box 048, except for businesses in the trucking industry, which face penalties starting with 2025 slips. The duty to report has not gone away, and the CRA uses these slips to check that contractors report their income.

T5018 slips: the rule for construction businesses

If your business's main activity is construction, meaning more than half of your business income comes from it, you report payments to subcontractors on a T5018, Statement of Contract Payments, instead of a T4A.

  • What to report: payments to Canadian-resident subcontractors for construction services, whether paid by cheque, cash, barter or offset. Payments for goods only are left out.
  • Threshold: you don't need to report a subcontractor you paid $500 or less in the year (not counting GST/HST). On the slip itself, the amount reported includes GST/HST and PST, which is different from the T4A.
  • Reporting period: you can report on a calendar year or on your business's fiscal year.
  • Due date: six months after the end of the reporting period you chose. If you use the 2026 calendar year, that works out to June 30, 2027.
  • Copies: you file with the CRA. You don't have to give subcontractors a copy, though you may.

Only report subcontractors you hired directly. A business that does some construction on the side but whose main business is something else does not need to file T5018s; it uses the T4A for service fees instead.

Filing online

If you file more than 5 slips for a calendar year, you must file electronically, for example through your accounting software or the CRA's online services. Filing on paper when you should have filed online carries its own penalty, starting at $125 for 6 to 50 slips.

Late filing penalties

The CRA can charge a penalty for a late T4A return. It starts at $100 and grows with the number of slips and the number of days late, up to $7,500. Separately, not giving T4A slips to recipients on time can cost $25 a day, from $100 up to $2,500. Interest can be added on top.

GST/HST on contractor invoices

Many contractors add GST to their invoices, but not all of them have to. A contractor is a small supplier if their taxable sales were $30,000 or less over the last four calendar quarters. Small suppliers don't have to register for GST/HST, although they can choose to. Once they go over $30,000, they must register and start charging GST.

What this means for you:

  • If your business is registered for GST/HST, you can usually claim back the GST you pay on contractor invoices for your business as an input tax credit (ITC). That is a credit on your GST return for tax you paid on business costs.
  • To claim it, keep invoices that show the contractor's GST/HST number. You can check that the number is real with the CRA's free GST/HST Registry, using the number, the business name and the invoice date.
  • If a contractor charges GST but isn't registered, you can't claim an ITC for it.
  • Most businesses must claim ITCs within four years.

WorkSafeBC: protect yourself with a clearance letter

WorkSafeBC provides workers' compensation insurance in B.C. Whether you must cover a contractor depends on how independent they are.

  • Independent businesses (for example, they have several clients, advertise to the public and bring their own major equipment) are generally not treated as your workers. They handle their own WorkSafeBC registration.
  • Labour-only contractors who don't run an independent business may count as your workers. In that case you are responsible for their coverage and must pay premiums for them.
  • A sole proprietor or partner is not automatically covered for their own injuries. They can buy optional Personal Coverage. WorkSafeBC can't tell you whether they have it, so ask the contractor for proof.

If you hire a contractor who is registered with WorkSafeBC but isn't paying their premiums, you could be held liable for the premiums tied to the work they did for you. A clearance letter confirms a business is registered and in good standing. Get one before and after the contractor works for you. Most letters are available through WorkSafeBC's online clearance letter tool using the firm's account number or exact name. The letter must be addressed to you to count.

Common mistakes and a checklist

Common mistakes

  • Calling someone a contractor when you control their hours, tools and duties like an employer.
  • Waiting until February to ask for a SIN or business number.
  • Including GST/HST in box 048 of the T4A.
  • A construction business sending T4As instead of T5018s, or the other way around.
  • Filing more than 5 slips on paper.
  • Skipping the WorkSafeBC clearance letter for a contractor who works on your job.
  • Claiming an ITC on GST from a contractor whose GST number you never checked.

Checklist

  1. Confirm the person is really a contractor; ask the CRA for a ruling if unsure.
  2. Collect their legal name, address, SIN or BN, and GST/HST number.
  3. Put the work, price and responsibilities in a written agreement.
  4. Get a WorkSafeBC clearance letter before and after the work.
  5. Track payments to each contractor through the year, without GST/HST (with it, for T5018 amounts).
  6. Decide whether you file T4As (most businesses) or T5018s (construction businesses).
  7. For 2026 payments, file T4A slips and the summary, and give contractors their copies, by February 28, 2027 (on time by March 1, 2027).
  8. File online if you have more than 5 slips.
