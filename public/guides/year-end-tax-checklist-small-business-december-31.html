Year-end tax checklist for small businesses: what to do before December 31, 2026

A few weeks of planning in December can save a small business real money and a lot of stress at tax time. This guide walks sole proprietors and small corporations in B.C. through the tax steps that are easiest, or only possible, to take before December 31, 2026, and the deadlines that follow early in 2027.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 20, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Equipment counts for 2026 only if it is available for use (installed and working) by your year-end, not just ordered.
  • Budget 2026 proposes letting most new equipment bought from September 15, 2026 be written off in full in year one. It is not law yet.
  • Owner-managers: decide your 2026 salary and dividends now. Salary creates RRSP room; dividends don't.
  • A shareholder loan from your company must be repaid within one year after the company's year-end, or it can be taxed as your income.
  • 2026 T4 and T4A slips are due by Monday, March 1, 2027, so check employee and contractor details in December.
  • Reconcile your accounts to December 31, gather receipts and record your vehicle's odometer reading on the last day of the year.

Why December 31 matters for your business

Most sole proprietors (people who run a business in their own name, without a company) use the calendar year as their business year. That means December 31, 2026 is the last day for anything you want counted in your 2026 business income, which you report on form T2125 with your personal tax return.

A corporation can choose its own year-end, which it sets when it files its first tax return. Many small companies use December 31, and this guide is written with that date in mind. If your company's year-end is different, the same steps apply on your own year-end date, with two exceptions: payroll slips and the personal side of salary and dividends always follow the calendar year, because they end up on your personal return.

Looking for the personal side (donations, FHSA, TFSA timing and investment losses)? See our year-end tax checklist for individuals.

Plan, don't panic-buy. A tax deduction only saves part of what you spend. Buying something you don't need just to get a deduction still leaves you out of pocket. Use the steps below for spending and decisions you would make anyway.

Buying equipment, computers or software

When a business buys something that lasts for years, such as a computer, tools, furniture or machinery, it usually can't deduct the whole cost at once. Instead it claims capital cost allowance (CCA), which is tax depreciation: a percentage of the cost each year, depending on the asset's CCA class.

The key timing rule is that an asset generally counts for a year only once it is available for use: delivered, installed and ready to work. A machine ordered in December but installed in January belongs to next year. If timing matters to you, ask your supplier for a delivery date in writing and keep the invoice and delivery or installation record.

The proposed Productivity Mega Deduction

Budget 2026, tabled on November 5, 2026, includes the Productivity Mega Deduction. Under it, eligible investments acquired on or after September 15, 2026 could be fully deducted in the year they become available for use, instead of being spread over many years. The government says it would cover more than 65% of business investment and would be permanent.

Important limits:

  • It is a proposal. It needs Parliament to pass it before it becomes law.
  • It excludes some assets, mainly long-lived buildings, certain intangibles such as goodwill, certain passenger vehicles and regulated gas pipelines.
  • Used assets qualify only in limited cases, for example if neither you nor anyone related to you owned them before.

Our news article on the Productivity Mega Deduction explains the details. If you were planning to buy equipment in early 2027 anyway, it may be worth having it in use before December 31 instead. Talk to your accountant about whether your purchase would qualify.

Owner-managers: decide your 2026 salary and dividends now

If you own a corporation, you normally pay yourself through salary (which goes through payroll and gets a T4) or dividends (a share of the company's after-tax profit, reported on a T5), or a mix of both. Our guide to paying yourself from a corporation explains how they compare. At year-end, three timing points matter:

  • Salary and dividends are taxed to you in the year you receive them. A dividend paid on December 30, 2026 goes on your 2026 return; one paid on January 4, 2027 goes on your 2027 return. Decide which year suits you before the holidays.
  • Salary creates RRSP room; dividends don't. Your new RRSP room each year is 18% of the previous year's earned income, up to a dollar limit. The CRA's RRSP dollar limit for 2027 is $35,390. To earn the full 2027 room from salary alone, you would need about $196,600 of earned income in 2026 ($35,390 ÷ 18%).
  • A bonus can be deducted this year but paid next year, within limits. If the company records a bonus as an expense for 2026, the Income Tax Act requires it to be paid within 180 days after the company's year-end. If it isn't, the company can only deduct it in the year it is finally paid. For a December 31 year-end, that means paying it by late June 2027, with source deductions remitted as usual.
Example (illustration only). Priya owns a consulting company with a December 31 year-end. In December 2026 she decides on a $20,000 bonus for 2026. The company records it as a 2026 expense and pays it in March 2027, well within 180 days. The company deducts it on its 2026 return, and Priya reports it on her 2027 personal return, because that's when she received it.

Clear up shareholder loans

If you took money out of your company that wasn't salary, a dividend or an expense reimbursement, it is usually recorded as a shareholder loan. The Income Tax Act generally treats a loan from a company to its shareholder as income to the shareholder, unless it fits an exception.

The most common exception: the loan is repaid within one year after the end of the company's tax year in which it was made, and the repayment isn't part of a series of loans and repayments. For a company with a December 31 year-end, money borrowed during 2026 has to be repaid by December 31, 2027.

Before year-end:

  • Ask your bookkeeper for your shareholder loan balance as of December 31.
  • Look for personal expenses paid with the company card. They usually end up in this account.
  • If a balance from 2025 is still owing, plan how you will clear it before your 2026 year-end. Common ways are a repayment, a salary or a dividend, each with its own tax result.

Repaying with a new loan from the company just before the deadline usually doesn't work, because of the "series of loans and repayments" rule.

Payroll and contractor slips

Payroll always follows the calendar year, whatever your company's year-end.

  • T4 and T4A slips for 2026 are due by the last day of February. Because February 28, 2027 is a Sunday, the CRA's due date becomes the next business day: Monday, March 1, 2027. You must file the slips with the CRA and give copies to employees and recipients by then.
  • Check employee details in December: names, addresses and social insurance numbers. Missing or wrong details are the most common cause of slip problems.
  • Contractors: list who you paid in 2026 for services and whether you need to issue T4A or T5018 slips. Our guide to contractors, T4A and T5018 slips explains who needs one.
  • Staff gifts and parties: non-cash gifts are tax-free up to $500 per employee for the year, and a party open to all staff is tax-free up to $150 per person. Cash and most gift cards that work like cash are always taxable. The gift policy doesn't cover relatives or shareholders who work in the business, so gifts to yourself or family members on payroll don't get this exemption. See our article on holiday gifts and parties.
  • New rates from January 1, 2027: the base CPP rate goes down and the CPP and EI earnings ceilings go up. Make sure your payroll software is updated for the first 2027 pay run. Our article on 2027 CPP and EI numbers has the figures.

GST/HST: check your registration status

You generally have to register for the GST/HST once you are no longer a small supplier. A small supplier is a business whose taxable sales (together with associated businesses) were $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter and over the last four consecutive calendar quarters. Commercial ride-sharing drivers must register from their first fare, whatever their sales.

At year-end:

  • Add up your taxable sales for each quarter of 2026. If you passed $30,000 and aren't registered, deal with it now rather than at tax time.
  • If you are registered, collect the receipts that support your input tax credits (the GST you paid on business purchases, which you can claim back). Each receipt should show the supplier's GST number.
  • Annual filers with a December 31 year-end: diary your 2026 return and payment dates now.

Our guide to GST/HST and PST for small businesses in B.C. explains registration, rates and returns.

Get your books ready

Clean books on December 31 make the tax return faster, cheaper and more accurate. Before the holidays, or in the first week of January:

  • Reconcile every business bank account and credit card to the December 31 statement. Reconciling means matching each transaction in your books to the statement.
  • Gather receipts for 2026 expenses and file them with your records. Our bookkeeping and record-keeping guide covers what to keep and for how long.
  • Review unpaid invoices. Chase what you can before year-end. If you already reported an invoice as income and it can't be collected, talk to your accountant about writing it off as a bad debt.
  • Count your inventory on December 31 if you sell goods.
  • Record your vehicle's odometer reading on December 31. The CRA asks for the reading at the start and end of the business year, along with a log of business trips (date, destination, purpose and kilometres). After one full year of logs, you can use a three-month sample logbook in later years, as long as business use stays within 10% of the base year.
  • Separate personal spending that went through the business account, and the other way round.

Sole proprietors who pay instalments: your last 2026 instalment is due on December 15, 2026. See our article on the December 15 instalment deadline.

Checklist

  • Decide on any equipment purchases and make sure they are installed and working by December 31.
  • Owner-managers: set your 2026 salary, bonus and dividends, and remember salary creates RRSP room.
  • Check your shareholder loan balance and plan any repayment.
  • Confirm employee and contractor details for T4, T4A and T5018 slips due March 1, 2027.
  • Keep staff gifts within $500 and parties within $150 per person.
  • Update payroll software for 2027 CPP and EI rates.
  • Check your GST/HST status and gather receipts for input tax credits.
  • Reconcile bank and credit card accounts to December 31.
  • Count inventory and record your vehicle's odometer reading on December 31.
  • Pay your December 15 instalment if you have one.
