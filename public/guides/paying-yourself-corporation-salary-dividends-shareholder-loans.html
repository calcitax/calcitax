Paying yourself from your corporation: salary, dividends and shareholder loans

Once your business is incorporated, the money it earns belongs to the company, not to you. To get it into your own pocket, you need to choose how: a salary, dividends, or (carefully) a shareholder loan. Each one is taxed differently and comes with its own paperwork. This guide explains the options in plain language for owners of small BC corporations.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 9, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • Company money isn't yours: every withdrawal should be recorded as salary, a dividend or a loan.
  • Salary is deductible to the company, needs payroll and a T4, pays both halves of CPP and creates RRSP room.
  • Dividends come from after-tax profit, need a T5, and build no CPP pension or RRSP room.
  • Most dividends from small-business profit are non-eligible: grossed up 15%, then reduced by the dividend tax credit.
  • A shareholder loan not repaid within one year after the company's year-end can be added to your income.
  • T4 and T5 returns are due by the last day of February; an accrued bonus must be paid within 180 days of year-end.

First, the basics: the company's money isn't yours

When you incorporate, the corporation becomes a separate legal "person." You own it as a shareholder, which means you hold shares in it. If you also run it day to day, you are an owner-manager. Most small BC companies are Canadian-controlled private corporations (CCPCs): private companies controlled by Canadian residents.

Because the company is separate from you, the money in its bank account belongs to the company. You can't simply move it to your own account the way a sole proprietor takes "drawings." Every dollar you take out needs a label. The three most common are:

  • Salary or bonus: you are paid as an employee of your company.
  • Dividends: you are paid as a shareholder, out of the company's after-tax profit.
  • Shareholder loans: you borrow from the company and pay it back.

Each one is taxed differently, has its own paperwork and affects things like your Canada Pension Plan (CPP) and RRSP room. If you're still deciding whether to incorporate, start with our guide to sole proprietorship vs incorporation in BC.

Option 1: Paying yourself a salary

With a salary, your company treats you like any other employee. It runs payroll, takes deductions off each pay and sends them to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). At year-end it gives you a T4 slip, the form that shows your employment income and deductions.

What the company must do

  • Open a payroll account with the CRA and deduct income tax and CPP from each pay. Our guide to hiring your first employee walks through the set-up.
  • Send the deductions on time. Most new employers are "regular remitters," with remittances due on the 15th of the month after you were paid.
  • File the T4 return by the last day of February after the year ends.

CPP: you pay both halves

CPP is the public pension plan. Employees and employers each pay the same amount. When you own the company, both halves come out of money you control. For 2026:

  • The employee and employer each pay 5.95% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, up to a maximum of $4,230.45 each.
  • On earnings between $74,600 and $85,000, each also pays a second contribution (often called CPP2) of 4%, up to $416 each.

That is a real cost today, but it also builds your future CPP pension.

Employment Insurance (EI) is different. The CRA says a job is generally not insurable when the employee controls more than 40% of the company's voting shares. So many owner-managers don't pay EI premiums, and they also can't collect regular EI benefits from that job.

Why owners choose salary

  • It's a business expense. A reasonable salary lowers the company's taxable income.
  • It creates RRSP room. RRSP room is the amount you can put into a registered retirement savings plan and deduct. It's generally 18% of last year's "earned income," up to a yearly maximum. Salary counts as earned income. The maximum is $33,810 for 2026 and $35,390 for 2027.
  • It shows steady income, which can help when you apply for a mortgage or other loan.

Bonuses and "accrued" salary: the 180-day rule

Some owners wait until the company's year-end, see how much profit there is, and then declare a bonus. The company can record the bonus as an expense for that year even if it's paid a little later. This is called an accrued bonus.

There is a time limit. Under the Income Tax Act, if salary or a bonus is still unpaid 180 days after the end of the company's tax year, the company can't deduct it in that year. It can only deduct it in the year it's actually paid.

Illustration only: Your company's year ends on December 31, 2026. It records a $20,000 bonus for you. To deduct it in 2026, the company needs to actually pay it (with payroll deductions sent to the CRA) within 180 days after December 31, 2026.

Tip: An unpaid bonus isn't money in your pocket yet. Count the days from your company's year-end, and make sure payroll deductions on the bonus are remitted on time too.

Option 2: Paying yourself dividends

A dividend is a share of the company's profit paid to its shareholders. It comes out of profit the company has already paid tax on. The company reports dividends on a T5 slip and must file the T5 return, and give you your copy, by the last day of February after the year ends. A slip isn't needed if one person's total for the year is under $50.

What's different about dividends

  • Not a business expense. Dividends don't lower the company's taxable income.
  • No payroll. No CPP is paid, so there's no CPP pension built on that income. There are no source deductions, so you may owe tax when you file your own return.
  • No RRSP room. Dividends aren't on the CRA's list of earned income.

Gross-up and dividend tax credit

The tax system tries to avoid taxing the same profit twice, once in the company and again in your hands. It does this in two steps:

  1. Gross-up: you report more than you actually received. This "grossed-up" figure is called the taxable amount.
  2. Dividend tax credit: you then get a credit that lowers your tax, to make up for the tax the company already paid.

Eligible vs non-eligible dividends

There are two kinds of dividends:

  • Non-eligible dividends (the CRA calls them "other than eligible") usually come from profit taxed at the low small-business rate. Most dividends from a small owner-managed company are this kind. They are grossed up by 15%.
  • Eligible dividends generally come from profit taxed at the higher general rate. A CCPC tracks this in its general rate income pool (GRIP), and it can pay eligible dividends only up to that pool without extra tax. They are grossed up by 38%.

Illustration only: You receive a $10,000 non-eligible dividend in 2026. You report a taxable amount of $11,500. Your federal dividend tax credit is 9.0301% of that, about $1,038. The BC credit for 2026 is 1.96% of the taxable amount, about $225. Your actual tax depends on your other income.

Watch out for family dividends: Paying dividends to a spouse or adult children who don't work in the business can trigger the tax on split income (TOSI), a special tax on some income from a family business. Some exceptions apply, so check before you set up family shareholders.

Using a mix of salary and dividends

Many owner-managers take some salary and some dividends. There's no single right answer. The best mix depends on how much cash you need, whether you want CPP and RRSP room, your other income, and how much profit you'd like to leave in the company.

It helps to know how the company itself is taxed. A CCPC can claim the small business deduction, which lowers the tax rate on its active business income up to a "business limit" of $500,000 a year. For BC companies, that means a combined rate of 11% (9% federal plus 2% BC) on income up to the limit, and 27% (15% plus 12%) above it. The limit is shared between associated companies and shrinks once the company's investment income goes over $50,000 a year.

A few general points to discuss with your accountant:

  • Salary lowers company profit. Paying a salary can keep the company's income within the small-business limit.
  • Retirement goals matter. If you want RRSP room or a larger CPP pension, some salary helps.
  • Cash flow matters. Dividends avoid monthly payroll remittances, but you may need to set money aside for tax in April, or make tax instalments.
  • Personal expenses aren't a third option. If the company pays your personal bills, the CRA can treat that as a shareholder benefit that you must add to your income, and the company can't deduct it. See our guide to business expenses and deductions for what counts as a real business cost.

Shareholder loans: borrowing from your company

Your accountant keeps a shareholder loan account (sometimes called a shareholder account) in the company's books. It tracks money moving between you and the company:

  • If you put your own money in, or pay company bills personally, the company owes you. You can usually take that money back without tax.
  • If you take out more than the company owes you, you owe the company. That's a shareholder loan.

The one-year repayment rule

Under section 15(2) of the Income Tax Act, a loan from a corporation to a shareholder is generally added to the shareholder's income. But there is an important exception: the rule doesn't apply if the loan is repaid within one year after the end of the company's tax year in which you borrowed it.

Illustration only: Your company's year ends on December 31. You borrow $15,000 in March 2026. The loan arose in the company's 2026 tax year, so you'd need to repay it by December 31, 2027. If you don't, the $15,000 can be added to your 2026 income.

The repayment must be genuine. The Act says the exception doesn't apply if the repayment is part of a "series of loans or other transactions and repayments," for example repaying just before year-end and borrowing the same money back right after. Keep bank records that show the money actually moved back to the company.

The deemed interest benefit

Even a loan you repay on time can create a small taxable amount. If the loan carries no interest, or low interest, you're treated as receiving a benefit equal to interest at the CRA's prescribed rate, minus any interest you actually pay to the company no later than 30 days after the end of the year. The prescribed rate for this purpose is 3% for October 1 to December 31, 2026. The CRA sets it every three months.

Exceptions, briefly

Some loans made to someone because they are an employee, not because they own shares, are treated differently, for example a loan to help buy a home or a car used for work. There must also be real repayment terms. These exceptions are narrow and rarely help an owner who borrows because they control the company.

Warning: A shareholder loan is not a way to avoid tax. If it isn't repaid in time, you pay tax on the full amount, and you can't fix it with a journal entry. Plan repayment before you borrow.

Paperwork and year-end timing

Good records protect you if the CRA asks questions. Keep these in your company's minute book or files:

  • Directors' resolutions for dividends. A resolution is a short written decision by the company's directors. It should state the amount, the date, which class of shares, and whether any part is an eligible dividend.
  • Payroll records: pay stubs, remittance confirmations and T4s.
  • A shareholder loan schedule: dates and amounts of every amount borrowed or repaid, with bank proof, and any loan agreement.

Timing matters too. A few weeks before your company's year-end, review profit, decide whether to declare a bonus or dividend, and check any shareholder loan balance against its repayment date. Remember the slips: T4 and T5 returns for a calendar year are due by the last day of February, even if the company's tax year ends at a different time.

Common mistakes

  • Taking money with no label. Transfers that aren't recorded as salary, dividends or a loan repayment end up in the shareholder loan account and can become taxable.
  • Missing the one-year repayment deadline on a shareholder loan, or repaying and re-borrowing in a pattern.
  • Paying a bonus late. Unpaid after 180 days, the company loses the deduction for that year.
  • Skipping payroll on salary. Salary needs source deductions, CPP and a T4. Late remittances are charged interest.
  • Forgetting the T5. Dividends need a T5 slip by the last day of February.
  • Not saving for personal tax on dividends. No tax is withheld, so set money aside.
  • Running personal bills through the company. These can become shareholder benefits that the company can't deduct.
  • Paying family dividends without checking TOSI.
