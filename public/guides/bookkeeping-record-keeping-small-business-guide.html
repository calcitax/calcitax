Bookkeeping and record-keeping basics for small businesses in BC

Good records are the backbone of a healthy small business. They help you claim every expense, file accurate returns and handle a CRA review calmly. Here's what to keep, how long to keep it and a simple routine that keeps your books under control.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 15, 2026

In short

  • Keep a record of every sale and every business expense, backed up by original documents like invoices, receipts and bank statements.
  • The CRA generally expects you to keep records for six years from the end of the last tax year they relate to; BC asks for PST records to be kept for five years.
  • You can scan paper receipts, but you may only throw away the originals if your scans meet the CRA's imaging standard.
  • To claim GST input tax credits, your receipts must show more details as the purchase gets bigger, with steps at $100 and $500.
  • Most businesses must use the accrual method, which means reporting income when you earn it, not when you are paid.
  • A short monthly routine (sort receipts, record transactions, match to the bank) saves hours at tax time.

What bookkeeping is and why it matters

Bookkeeping means recording every money transaction your business makes: each sale, each purchase, each payment and each deposit. Your records are the organized summaries of those transactions (such as a ledger, which is a list of transactions sorted by type) plus the supporting documents that prove them, such as invoices, receipts, bank statements and emails about a sale.

Keeping records is not optional. Anyone who carries on a business, files a GST/HST return or deducts payroll amounts must keep them. This includes inactive corporations and holding companies.

Good records help you claim every expense and input tax credit you are entitled to, see whether you should be charging GST, get through a CRA review faster, and know whether your business is making a profit.

If your records are incomplete, CRA auditors may use other methods to estimate your income, and any expenses you cannot support could be denied. If you ever receive a review letter, our guide to CRA letters, reviews and audits explains what to expect.

What records to keep

The CRA does not require a particular bookkeeping system, but your records must be complete, reliable, supported by documents, and kept in English, French or both.

Income (sales) records

Track your gross income, which is your total income before you subtract any expenses. For each entry, note the date, the amount and where the money came from. Record it even when you are paid in goods or services. Back it up with sales invoices, cash register tapes, receipts, deposit slips, fee statements and contracts.

Expense records

Get a receipt for every business purchase. Ideally it shows the date, the seller's and your names and addresses, and a full description of the purchase. If a receipt has no description (a till slip, for example), write one on it. If you got no receipt at all, write down the seller's name and address, the amount, the date and what it was for. Our guide to self-employed expenses and deductions covers what you can claim.

Bank records

Keep your bank statements, duplicate deposit slips and cancelled cheques.

Property and equipment

For bigger items you use for years, like a computer, tools or a vehicle, record who you bought it from, the cost and the purchase date. When you sell or trade it in, record the date and the amount you got. These records support your capital cost allowance, which is the yearly tax deduction for the wear and tear on those items.

Vehicle logbook

If you use a vehicle for business, the best proof is a logbook kept for the whole year. For each business trip, write down the date, where you went, why, and how many kilometres you drove. Also note the odometer reading at the start and end of each year. After one full year of logging (your base year), the CRA lets you keep a three-month sample logbook in later years, as long as your business use stays within 10% of the base year.

Payroll records

If you have employees, keep each person's hours worked, the income tax, CPP and EI you withheld, their TD1 forms (the personal tax credits form every employee fills in), and copies of every slip and return you file. See our guide to hiring your first employee, and for payments to contractors, our guide to T4A and T5018 slips.

Online sales

Keep order confirmations and emails about each sale yourself. Online stores and payment platforms may not keep them as long as the law requires.

Receipts for GST input tax credits and BC PST records

If you are registered for GST, you can get back the GST you pay on business purchases by claiming input tax credits (ITCs). To claim them, you need receipts or invoices with the right details. The bigger the purchase, the more details the CRA requires:

Total purchaseWhat the receipt or invoice must show
Under $100Supplier's business name, the date, and the total amount
$100 to $499.99All of the above, plus the GST/HST charged (or a note that it is included in the price) and the supplier's GST/HST registration number
$500 or moreAll of the above, plus your business name, a short description of what you bought, and the payment terms

For most small businesses, ITCs can be claimed up to about four years after the period in which you could first have claimed them. Your own sales invoices to GST-registered customers need the same details.

BC PST records

If you are registered to collect BC's provincial sales tax (PST), the province expects records of:

  • all sales and leases, whether taxable or not,
  • all PST you charged, collected and sent to the province,
  • purchases and leases, both for resale and for your own use,
  • items you took out of inventory for your own use,
  • proof of why you did not charge PST on a taxable sale (for example, an exemption), and
  • refunds, credits and price reductions.

Our GST, HST and PST guide explains when you must register for each tax.

How long and where to keep your records

The CRA's general rule is to keep your records for six years from the end of the last tax year they relate to. For a corporation, the tax year is its fiscal period; for a sole proprietor, it is the calendar year. The same general rule applies to income tax, GST/HST and payroll records.

Illustration only: a sole proprietor's 2026 receipts for office supplies relate to the 2026 tax year, so they would generally be kept until the end of 2032. For equipment you deduct over several years, the six years count from the last year the records are needed.

Exceptions to know

  • Late-filed returns: keep the records for six years from the date you filed.
  • Objections or appeals: keep records until the dispute is settled and the time for any further appeal has passed, if that is later than the six years.
  • Long-term records: keep records about buying and selling long-term property, the share register and other history that affects selling or winding up the business indefinitely.
  • Closed sole proprietorships: keep records for six years from the end of the tax year the business ended.
  • Dissolved corporations: keep records until two years after the corporation is dissolved.
  • BC PST: the province asks you to keep PST records for five years, or longer if an appeal is still outstanding.

Because federal and provincial periods differ, the simplest approach is to keep everything for at least six years. Thinking of incorporating? See our guide to sole proprietor vs incorporation.

Don't shred early without permission. To destroy records before the six years are up, you need written permission from the CRA. You can ask using Form T137, Request for Destruction of Records, or by writing to your tax services office. Destroying records without permission can lead to prosecution.

Where to keep them

Keep your records at your place of business or your home in Canada, unless the CRA gives you written permission to keep them elsewhere. Records stored on a server outside Canada do not count as kept in Canada just because you can open them from here. If a bookkeeper or accountant holds your records, you are still responsible for them.

Paper, scanned and electronic records

The CRA accepts paper records, paper records turned into electronic images, and records that were electronic from the start. A few rules apply:

  • Electronic records stay electronic. If a record was created digitally, such as an emailed invoice or your accounting software file, you must keep it in a readable electronic format, even if you also print it.
  • Scanning paper receipts. Generally you must keep the original paper. You can keep scanned images instead only if they are accurate copies with no important details hidden, and are made and kept according to the national imaging standard the CRA refers to (CAN/CGSB 72.34). If you cannot meet that standard, keep the paper originals too.
  • Back up your files. Keep backup copies, ideally at a location in Canada separate from your business, and make sure they can still be opened if you change software.
Tip: Before switching to a new bookkeeping app or cancelling an old subscription, export your past years' data in a format you can still open. The CRA can ask to see records from past years, and you are responsible for them even if a software provider stops keeping them.

Setting up your system: accrual basics, bank accounts and software

Cash vs accrual

There are two main ways to time your income and expenses:

  • Accrual method: you report income when you earn it (for example, when you send an invoice), even if you have not been paid yet, and you deduct expenses when you incur them, even if you have not paid them yet.
  • Cash method: you report income when you receive the money and deduct expenses when you pay them.

For self-employment income, the CRA requires the accrual method unless you are a farmer, fisher or self-employed commission agent, who may choose either.

Illustration only: you finish a $2,000 job and invoice the client on December 20, 2026, and they pay you on January 10, 2027. Under the accrual method, that $2,000 is 2026 income.

Use a separate business bank account

The CRA does not require a separate account, but it is one of the easiest ways to keep clean books. Mixed personal and business spending has to be sorted by hand and makes business expenses harder to prove. A dedicated account and credit card give you a ready-made list of business transactions.

Spreadsheet or software?

A business with a few transactions a month can manage with a tidy spreadsheet. Once you have regular sales, sales tax, inventory or employees, bookkeeping software usually saves time. Whatever you choose, you should be able to trace any number in your financial statements back to the original receipt or invoice. The CRA calls this an audit trail. Keep a separate set of records for each business you run.

A simple monthly bookkeeping routine

The CRA suggests keeping a daily record of income and expenses. In practice, a short regular routine works well for most small businesses:

  1. Every week: collect receipts (paper and email) in one place, write a short note on any that are unclear, and send out invoices for finished work.
  2. Every month: record all sales and expenses, sorting each into a category such as supplies, rent, phone or vehicle.
  3. Reconcile your bank and credit card. Reconciling means checking that every transaction on your statement matches an entry in your books, and fixing anything that doesn't.
  4. Review unpaid invoices and bills and follow up.
  5. Set aside sales tax and payroll deductions you have collected. That money belongs to the government, not your business.
  6. Back up your files and file away the month's documents.

At year-end, count and list any inventory and record your vehicle odometer readings.

Common mistakes and a checklist

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing personal and business spending in one account.
  • Keeping only bank or card statements, with no receipts showing what was bought.
  • Missing the supplier's GST number on receipts of $100 or more, so input tax credits can't be supported.
  • Rebuilding a vehicle logbook from memory at year-end.
  • Throwing out paper receipts after scanning them without meeting the imaging standard.
  • Shredding records before six years are up, or losing them when changing software.

Checklist

  1. Open a separate business bank account.
  2. Choose a spreadsheet or bookkeeping software and set up expense categories.
  3. Keep every sales invoice, receipt, bank statement and contract.
  4. Check that receipts have the details needed for GST input tax credits.
  5. Keep a vehicle logbook for business trips.
  6. Reconcile your bank and credit card accounts every month.
  7. Store records in Canada, with backups, for at least six years.
  8. Ask the CRA in writing before destroying any records early.
Remember: even if you hire a bookkeeper or accountant, you remain responsible for your records. Ask for copies of your files each year so you always have them.
