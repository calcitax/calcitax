Canada child benefit and B.C. family benefit explained

The Canada child benefit and the B.C. family benefit put tax-free money in parents' bank accounts every month. Here's who qualifies, how to apply, how much you could get from July 2026 to June 2027, and the simple steps that keep the payments coming.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 3, 2026 · Figures for July 2026 to June 2027

In short

  • The CCB pays up to $8,157 a year for a child under 6 and $6,883 for a child aged 6 to 17 (July 2026 to June 2027).
  • The B.C. family benefit adds up to $1,750 for a first child and is paid with the CCB, with no separate application.
  • Payments are based on your family's net income from the year before, so you and your spouse must both file every year, even with no income.
  • In B.C., you can apply for the CCB when you register your baby's birth; otherwise use your CRA account or Form RC66.
  • With shared custody (40% to 60% of the time), each parent gets half.
  • Report changes in address, marital status and custody quickly to avoid paying money back.

What these benefits are

The Canada child benefit (CCB) is a monthly payment from the federal government to help families with the cost of raising children under 18. It is tax-free, which means you don't report it as income and it doesn't raise your tax bill. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) runs it.

The B.C. family benefit is a separate payment from the Province of British Columbia for B.C. families with children under 18. It is also tax-free. The province pays for it, but the CRA works out the amount and adds it to your CCB, so you get one combined deposit each month.

Three terms come up again and again:

  • Benefit year: payments run from July to the following June. This guide uses figures for July 2026 to June 2027.
  • Base year: the tax year the CRA uses to set your payments. For July 2026 to June 2027, it is your 2025 income, from the returns you filed in spring 2026.
  • Adjusted family net income (AFNI): your net income from your tax return plus your spouse's or common-law partner's, with a few small adjustments, such as taking out income from a registered disability savings plan (RDSP). The lower your AFNI, the more you get.

Amounts go up with inflation each July, so the same family usually gets a little more each year.

Who qualifies

For each child, you can get the CCB if all of these are true:

  • the child is under 18 and lives with you;
  • you are primarily responsible for the child, meaning you look after their daily needs, make sure their medical needs are met and arrange child care when needed;
  • you are a resident of Canada for tax purposes (you normally live here and have strong ties, such as a home or family here); and
  • you or your spouse or common-law partner is a Canadian citizen, a permanent resident, a protected person, a person registered (or entitled to be registered) under the Indian Act, or a temporary resident who has lived in Canada for the previous 18 months and holds a valid permit in the 19th month.

Newcomers

A protected person is someone who has received a positive decision from the Immigration and Refugee Board. A refugee claimant who is still waiting for a decision does not qualify yet. Temporary residents, such as work-permit holders, must wait until the 19th month in Canada. If your permit is renewed, send the new permit to the CRA through your CRA account so payments continue. Our newcomers tax guide covers your first return.

Two parents in one home

Only one CCB payment goes to each household, and the amount is the same whichever parent receives it. When a mother and father live together, the CRA assumes the mother is primarily responsible, so she should usually apply. If the father is the main caregiver, he can apply with a signed letter from the mother. Same-sex couples choose one parent to apply for all the children.

Shared custody

If your child lives with you 40% to 60% of the time, you and the other parent have shared custody. You each apply, and you each get half of what you would get with full custody, based on your own household's income. If the child lives with you less than 40% of the time, you can't apply. More than 60% counts as full custody.

You can't get the CCB for a foster child in any month when the agency receives children's special allowances for that child.

How to apply

Apply as soon as your child is born, a child comes to live with you, or your custody arrangement starts or changes. There are three ways:

  1. When you register your baby's birth. In B.C., you can apply for the CCB at the same time as you register the birth online. You must give consent to share your information with the CRA, or the application isn't sent. You don't need to send proof of birth this way.
  2. Through your CRA account. Sign in, go to Benefits and credits and add the child under Child information. Upload any documents the CRA asks for.
  3. By mail with Form RC66, the Canada Child Benefits Application, if you can't use the other two options.

You don't apply separately for the B.C. family benefit. Once a child is registered for the CCB, they are automatically registered for the B.C. family benefit too. The same goes for the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (the payment that replaced the GST/HST credit): children you get the CCB for are counted automatically.

Extra paperwork in some cases

  • Newcomers and returning residents also send Schedule RC66SCH (status in Canada and income) with Form RC66.
  • If your spouse or partner was a non-resident for part of a year, Form CTB9 reports their income for that year.
  • If you are asking for payments for a period that started more than 11 months ago, expect to send proof of status, residence in Canada, the child's birth and that you are the main caregiver.
  • Documents not in English or French need a proper translation.
Tip: Ticking the benefits box when you register your newborn's birth is the fastest route. It also means you don't have to mail proof of birth.

How much CCB you can get

Here are the most you can get per child for July 2026 to June 2027. Families with AFNI under $38,237 get these full amounts.

Child's ageMaximum per yearMaximum per month
Under 6$8,157$679.75
6 to 17$6,883$573.58

How the amount goes down as income rises

The CCB shrinks gradually, in two stages. This is called a phase-out:

  • Stage one: on family income between $38,237 and $82,847, the yearly benefit drops by 7% of the income over $38,237 for one child, 13.5% for two children, 19% for three, and 23% for four or more.
  • Stage two: above $82,847, the drop slows down: 3.2% of the extra income for one child, 5.7% for two, 8% for three and 9.5% for four or more (on top of the stage-one reduction).

Because the second stage is gentle, many middle-income families still get something. You don't have to do the maths yourself: the CRA calculates it and sends a notice each summer. The CRA's online child and family benefits calculator gives an estimate.

Illustration only: a B.C. family with one child aged 3 and AFNI of $60,000. That is about $21,760 over the first threshold. 7% of that is about $1,520, so the family's CCB is about $8,157 - $1,520 = about $6,630 a year, or roughly $550 a month.

Child disability benefit

The child disability benefit is an extra amount added to the CCB for a child who qualifies for the disability tax credit (DTC), a credit for people with a severe and prolonged impairment. For July 2026 to June 2027 it is up to $3,480 a year ($290 a month) per child. It starts to go down when AFNI is over $82,847. To get it, a medical practitioner fills out Form T2201 and the CRA must approve it. Once approved, the CRA automatically looks back over the current and two previous benefit years. The B.C. family benefit

For July 2026 to June 2027, the B.C. family benefit is based on your 2025 AFNI.

ChildMaximum per yearMinimum per year (income $30,176 to $96,562)
First child$1,750$775
Second child$1,100$750
Each additional child$900$725

In plain words:

  • Under $30,176: you get the maximum.
  • $30,176 to $96,562: the benefit goes down by 4% of income over $30,176, but never below the minimum amounts in the table.
  • Over $96,562: the remaining amount goes down by 4% of income over $96,562 until it reaches zero.

Single parents with low income can get a supplement of up to $500 a year per family. It shrinks once AFNI is over $30,176. Parents with shared custody get half the amount for that child.

Illustration only: for the family above (one child, AFNI $60,000), 4% of the $29,824 over $30,176 is about $1,190. That would wipe out most of the $1,750, but the minimum protects them, so they get $775 a year (about $64.58 a month), added to their CCB deposit.

Coming in July 2027: a disability supplement

B.C. has announced a children and youth disability supplement of up to $6,000 a year per child approved for the DTC, to be paid with the B.C. family benefit starting in July 2027 and based on 2026 returns. The province describes it as proposed in its 2026 budget legislation, so details could still change. Families already approved for the DTC should not need to apply.

Moving out of B.C.? Tell the CRA right away. The B.C. family benefit is only for B.C. residents, and payments made after you move have to be paid back. Also, the province says it won't contact you by text about this benefit, so treat any such text as a likely scam.

Payment dates

The CCB and B.C. family benefit arrive together, usually around the 20th of each month. The remaining CCB dates for 2026 are:

  • November 20, 2026
  • December 11, 2026

The CRA had not yet posted its 2027 dates when we checked on November 3, 2026. We'll update this guide once they're published.

  • If your total for the year is under $240, you get one lump sum with your July payment instead of monthly payments.
  • If a payment doesn't arrive, check your CRA account and make sure your details are up to date. Wait 5 business days after the expected date before calling.
  • Direct deposit is the most reliable way to get paid. If you change banks, keep the old account open until the first payment lands in the new one.

Keeping your payments on track

File your taxes every year, both of you

You and your spouse or common-law partner must each file a tax return every year, even if you had no income or your income isn't taxable. The CRA can't work out your family income without both returns. File by April 30 so your new amount starts on time in July. If you file late, payments can stop for a while. You get the missed amounts once your return is assessed, but the gap can hurt a family budget.

Warning: A stay-at-home parent, a student spouse or a partner with only foreign income still needs to file. If a spouse lived outside Canada for part of the year, their world income counts too.

Tell the CRA about changes

  • Marital status: report a marriage, new common-law relationship or separation by the end of the month after the change. For a separation, wait until you have been apart for more than 90 days in a row.
  • Address: update it right away, even if you use direct deposit, or your payments may stop.
  • Custody: if a child moves in or out, or a shared custody arrangement starts, ends or changes, each parent should tell the CRA promptly.
  • Leaving Canada: tell the CRA if you or your spouse stop being a resident.
  • If a child dies: tell the CRA as soon as you can. For a death on or after January 1, 2026, the family may keep getting payments for up to 6 more months.

Your CRA account is the fastest way to make most changes.

Common mistakes and checklist

Common mistakes

  • Not filing a return because "I had no income", which stops payments for the whole family.
  • Waiting for the first tax return in Canada before applying. Newcomers can apply once they qualify.
  • Forgetting to send a renewed work or study permit to the CRA.
  • Both separated parents claiming full custody, or neither applying for shared custody. The CRA may later recalculate and ask for money back.
  • Reporting a reconciliation or new partner late. Any amount you were overpaid has to be repaid.
  • Never applying for the disability tax credit for a child who might qualify, missing both the child disability benefit and the coming B.C. supplement.
  • Not telling the CRA after moving out of B.C.

Checklist

  • Applied for the CCB at birth registration, online or with Form RC66 (plus RC66SCH if new to Canada).
  • Both spouses or partners filed returns by April 30, 2027 for the 2026 tax year.
  • Address, marital status and bank details are current in your CRA account.
  • Custody changes reported by each parent.
  • Asked about the disability tax credit (Form T2201) if your child has a serious, long-lasting condition.
  • Checked your benefit notice each summer to confirm the amounts.
