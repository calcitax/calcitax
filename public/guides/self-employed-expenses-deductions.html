Self-employed? What you can deduct on Form T2125

When you work for yourself, every legitimate business expense lowers the tax you pay. This guide explains what you can claim on Form T2125, the rules for home offices and vehicles, and the records you need to back it all up.

By Calcitax Advisors · Updated November 2, 2026 · Figures for 2026

In short

  • You can deduct reasonable expenses you incur to earn business income, but only the business share of anything you also use personally.
  • A home office qualifies only if it's your main place of business, or used only for business and regularly to meet clients, and it can't create a loss.
  • Vehicle costs are claimed by business kilometres, so keep a logbook; for 2026, the class 10.1 cost limit for passenger vehicles is $39,000 plus tax.
  • Meals and entertainment are usually only 50% deductible, and big purchases are claimed over time through capital cost allowance.
  • For 2026, self-employed people pay both halves of CPP: 11.9% up to a maximum of $8,460.90, plus CPP2.
  • File by June 15, 2027 for the 2026 tax year, but pay any balance by April 30, 2027.

The basic test: what makes an expense deductible

If you're a sole proprietor, freelancer, contractor, or ride-share or delivery driver, you report your business income and expenses on Form T2125, the Statement of Business or Professional Activities, which goes with your personal tax return. You pay tax only on your net income: what you earned minus your allowable expenses.

Two questions decide whether an expense counts:

  1. Did you spend it to earn business income? The CRA describes a business expense as a cost incurred "for the sole purpose of earning business income." Personal costs don't qualify.
  2. Is the amount reasonable? You can generally deduct reasonable current expenses. A $5,000 dinner with one client, or paying your teenager $40 an hour to file papers, would raise questions.

When something is used for both business and personal life, like a car, a phone or your home, you deduct only the business part. And if you're registered for GST/HST, you deduct each expense net of any input tax credit you claim (more on that below).

Common deductible expenses

These are the everyday costs most self-employed people can claim, as long as they're for the business:

  • Advertising: website, online ads, business cards, flyers.
  • Supplies used up in your work, and office expenses such as stationery, postage and printer ink.
  • Professional fees: accounting, bookkeeping and legal fees for the business.
  • Business taxes, licences and dues: a municipal business licence, professional association fees.
  • Insurance for the business, such as liability insurance.
  • Interest and bank charges on business loans, business credit cards and your business account.
  • Rent for an office, workshop or storage unit.
  • Delivery and freight costs.
  • Travel costs for business trips.
  • Salaries and wages paid to employees, including your spouse or child if they really do the work, you actually pay them, and the pay is what you'd pay a stranger for the same job. You must issue them T4 slips.

Meals and entertainment are deductible, but usually only 50% of the lesser of what you spent and a reasonable amount. So a $100 client lunch gives a $50 deduction. A few exceptions exist, for example if you bill the meal to a client and show it on the invoice. Couriers and rickshaw drivers can claim a flat $23 a day for the extra food they need on an eight-hour working day, as long as they keep a logbook.

Phone and internet: a separate business phone line is fully deductible. For your cellphone, claim the business percentage of your plan. The basic monthly rate of a personal home landline isn't deductible, but business long-distance calls are. Apply the same business-share idea to your internet.

Business use of your home

Working from home can let you claim part of your household costs, but only if your workspace meets one of these conditions:

  • it's your main place of business, or
  • you use it only to earn business income, and you use it regularly and on an ongoing basis to meet clients, customers or patients.

What you can include

  • Homeowners: a share of heating, electricity, home insurance, cleaning materials, maintenance, property taxes and mortgage interest (not the principal part of your payment).
  • Renters: a share of your rent and any expenses that relate to the workspace.

How to work out your share

Use a reasonable method, usually floor area. If your office is 150 square feet in a 1,500-square-foot home, your business share is 10%. If annual eligible costs are $24,000, you could claim $2,400.

If the room is also used by the family, multiply further by the hours it's used for business. For example, a room used for business 8 hours a day gives 8 ÷ 24 = one-third of that room's share. If you run the business for only part of the year, reduce the claim to match.

Limits to know

  • Home office expenses can't create or increase a business loss. You can claim them only up to your business income after other expenses.
  • Any amount you can't use carries forward to next year, under the same conditions.
  • You can also claim capital cost allowance (CCA, the tax version of depreciation) on the business part of a home you own, but this is usually a bad idea: when you sell, capital gains and recapture rules can apply to that business part of your home.

Car and vehicle expenses

If you use your vehicle for business, you can deduct the business share of its costs. That's especially important for ride-share, delivery and trades workers.

What you can claim

Fuel, or electricity for an electric vehicle, oil changes, maintenance and repairs, insurance, licence and registration, interest on a car loan, lease payments, and CCA if you own the vehicle. Parking for business trips and extra business insurance are deductible in full.

Business share = business kilometres ÷ total kilometres

Example: you drive 20,000 km in the year, and 15,000 km are for business. Your business share is 75%. If your vehicle costs (not counting CCA) total $8,000, you can claim $6,000.

Keep a logbook

For each business trip, record the date, destination, purpose and kilometres. Write down your odometer reading at the start and end of the year. After you've kept a full logbook for one year, the CRA allows a simpler option: a three-month sample logbook in later years, as long as your business use stays within 10% of that base year.

Limits on passenger vehicles for 2026

Most cars count as "passenger vehicles" (Guide T4002 has a chart to classify yours, based on seating and how it's used). For passenger vehicles, these 2026 limits apply:

Cost you can depreciate (CCA class 10.1), for vehicles bought on or after January 1, 2026$39,000 plus tax

Deductible car loan interest (new loans)$350 a month

The CRA's per-kilometre rate isn't for you. For 2026, the federal government set 73 cents a km for the first 5,000 km and 67 cents after that in the provinces. Those rates limit tax-free allowances that employers pay employees. As a self-employed person, you claim your actual costs times your business-use share, backed by receipts and a logbook.

Bigger purchases: capital cost allowance

Some costs give you a benefit for many years, like a laptop, tools, furniture or a vehicle. These are capital expenses, and you usually can't deduct them all at once. Instead, you claim capital cost allowance (CCA), a set percentage each year. Day-to-day costs and repairs that just restore something to how it was are current expenses and are deducted in full.

Some common CCA classes:

  • Class 50 (55%): computers and related hardware.
  • Class 8 (20%): furniture, equipment and tools costing $500 or more.
  • Class 12 (100%): small tools and kitchen utensils costing less than $500, plus most application software.
  • Class 10 (30%): motor vehicles, and passenger vehicles that cost $39,000 or less (for 2026 purchases).
  • Class 10.1 (30%): passenger vehicles that cost more than the limit. Each one goes in its own class, and you can only depreciate up to the limit.

In the year you buy something, the half-year rule usually lets you claim CCA on only half the cost. You don't have to claim the maximum CCA; you can claim less in a low-income year and save the balance for later.

A temporary rule that let unincorporated business owners write off some purchases right away ("immediate expensing") applied only to property that became available for use before 2025, so it doesn't help with purchases made now. The CRA's guide also describes proposed rules for a larger first-year claim on some property; ask before relying on them.

What you can't deduct

  • Personal and living expenses, such as your groceries, family trips, gym membership, or the personal share of your car, phone and home.
  • Money you pay yourself. Your own "salary" or drawings from a sole proprietorship aren't an expense.
  • Most fines and penalties, including parking and speeding tickets, even on a business trip.
  • Interest and penalties on your income tax.
  • Donations and political contributions.
  • Most life insurance premiums.
  • The full cost of capital items in one year (use CCA instead).
  • Clothing is a grey area. The CRA's lists of business expenses don't include everyday clothing, and personal costs can't be deducted, so ask before claiming any.

GST/HST and keeping records

GST/HST input tax credits

You must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales go over $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters. Taxi and ride-share drivers must register no matter how much they earn. Once registered, you can usually claim back the GST/HST you paid on business expenses as input tax credits (ITCs). If you do, deduct the expense on your T2125 without the GST/HST you got back, so you don't claim it twice.

Records and receipts

  • Keep receipts, invoices and bank and credit card statements. Receipts should show the seller's name and the date.
  • Keep your records for at least six years after the end of the last tax year they relate to.
  • Digital copies are fine if they are clear and easy to read.
  • Keep your vehicle logbook and your home-office measurements with your records.
Tip: a separate bank account and credit card for the business makes record keeping much easier, and makes it simple to show the CRA which costs were business costs.

CPP, instalments and deadlines

CPP for the self-employed

Employees pay half of their Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions and their employer pays the other half. When you're self-employed, you pay both halves, calculated on your net self-employment income when you file your return. For 2026:

  • 11.9% on net earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, up to a maximum of $8,460.90.
  • A second layer (CPP2) of 8% on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000, up to $832.

Example: with $50,000 of net self-employment income, your 2026 CPP is ($50,000 − $3,500) × 11.9% = $5,533.50.

There's some relief: the "employer" half of the base contributions, plus all the enhanced (extra) contributions, are deducted from your income. The other half of the base contributions gives you a non-refundable tax credit.

Instalments

If your net tax owing is more than $3,000 for this year, and was also over $3,000 in either of the two previous years, the CRA expects you to pay tax in instalments during the year. For 2027, the due dates are March 15, June 15, September 15 and December 15.

Filing and payment deadlines

For the 2026 tax year, self-employed people (and their spouses or common-law partners) have until June 15, 2027 to file. But any balance owing must be paid by April 30, 2027, or interest starts.

Common mistakes and a year-end checklist

Common mistakes

  • Claiming 100% of a car or phone that's also used personally.
  • No logbook, so the business share of driving can't be proven.
  • Claiming meals in full instead of 50%.
  • Deducting a laptop or vehicle in full instead of using CCA.
  • Using home office expenses to create a loss.
  • Claiming GST/HST as an expense and also as an input tax credit.
  • Filing on June 15 but paying late, and being charged interest from April 30.
  • Forgetting to set money aside for CPP, which can be a large bill for the self-employed.

Checklist before you file

  • Total income from all sources: invoices, platform statements and T4A slips.
  • Receipts sorted by category, with the GST/HST paid shown separately if you're registered.
  • Vehicle logbook and odometer readings for January 1 and December 31.
  • Home office measurements and the year's utility, insurance, property tax, mortgage interest or rent bills.
  • List of equipment bought or sold during the year, with dates and costs.
  • Any instalments paid, and money set aside for tax and CPP.
